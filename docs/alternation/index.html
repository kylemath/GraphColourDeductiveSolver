The 9-8 Alternation

Take a triangulated sphere and delete one vertex of degree five: the hole. Colour what is left with four colours. If the five neighbours around the hole (the rim) use all four colours, the hole cannot be filled. Kempe's old trick is to swap two colours along one connected two-colour chain. When both of his swaps are blocked (doubly locked), there is still one canonical swap, called π, and you can keep applying it.

Count every Kempe chain of the colouring, over all six colour pairs, and call the total N. Below is a real walk under π, taken from the exhaustive census of order 34. It is the longest stretch ever recorded in which every state is doubly locked with N ≤ 9. Along it N reads 9, 8, 9, 8, 9, 8, 9, 8. Step through it, and watch the beads.

Eight beads on the rim, plus the free ones

Think of each Kempe chain as a bead. At a doubly locked state the rim always strings exactly eight beads: one α–μ chain along the rim path, one A–B chain across the rim edge, two α–A and two α–B chains (one through each α), one μ–B chain and one μ–A chain. The last two are the locks. Every other bead floats free of the rim, so N = 8 + (free beads).

A rigid state has no free bead (N = 8). A state with N = 9 has exactly one. In the runs shown here that free bead is always an α–μ chain. On the sphere this is the σ-type lemma: a hand proof, independently reviewed, and checked at 26,481 of 26,481 such states in the census data.

Why every step is odd

The bead count carries a hidden clock. Read every triangle's three edge colours (as differences of colours in a 2×2 grid) and count the triangles that turn clockwise; call it cw. On the sphere, with n vertices,

2N ≡ cw + (n − 1) − η + 2(L1 + L2) (mod 4)

Here η depends only on the four rim colours, and L1, L2 are 1 when the locks are shut. One π-step always turns cw by exactly half a turn (2 mod 4) and leaves η alone. If both locks are still shut afterwards, the locks give back nothing, so 2N must move by 2 mod 4: the change in N is odd. If the step opens a lock, the change is even. This is the chain-parity law, proved by hand and compiled in Lean 4.

So from a rigid 8 the next doubly locked state cannot be 8. Staying at N ≤ 9 forces 8, 9, 8, 9, … exactly.

Why a loop needs at least ten

The rim pentagon reads α, μ, α, A, B, with the repeated colour α at positions j and j+2. Each π-step recolours the rim so that the pattern starts three places further round: j → j + 3 (mod 5). The page checks this at every step. Returning to the starting frame takes a multiple of 5 steps. The alternation needs an even number. A closed near-rigid loop would therefore need a multiple of 10 steps, so at least 10.

No such loop has ever been seen. The census covers every frame-class triangulation of orders 22 to 34 (3.6 million holes at order 34 alone), and the longest run is 8. Every run ends the same way: N jumps to 10 or more, or a lock opens and one swap fills the hole. Whether a loop can exist is the open claim NRC.

What is proved and what is not. The chain-count formula, the chain-parity law and rigid isolation (an 8 is never followed by an 8) are compiled in Lean 4 and audited. The eight-beads identity and the σ-type lemma are hand proofs with an independent AI review. NRC is open: no proof excludes a closed near-rigid loop. The census numbers are computations. This is not a proof of the Four Colour Theorem, which is already known.
