Framing the Problem

The Four Colour Theorem

Every map drawn on a plane (or equivalently, on a sphere) can be coloured using at most four colours such that no two adjacent regions — regions sharing a common boundary segment, not just a point — have the same colour.

Formal Statement

For every planar graph G:

χ(G) ≤ 4

where χ(G) denotes the chromatic number — the minimum number of colours needed to properly colour the vertices of G.

From Maps to Graphs

The Dual Graph

Every map can be converted to a dual graph:

  • Each region becomes a vertex
  • Two vertices are connected by an edge if and only if their regions share a boundary

This transformation is the bridge between cartography and graph theory. Colouring the map is equivalent to colouring the vertices of the dual graph.

Planarity

A graph is planar if it can be drawn in the plane without edge crossings. The Kuratowski-Wagner Theorem states that a graph is planar if and only if it does not contain K5 (the complete graph on 5 vertices) or K3,3 (the complete bipartite graph on 3+3 vertices) as a minor.

Euler's Formula & Colour Bounds

Euler's Formula

V − E + F = 2

For any connected planar graph embedded in the plane:

  • V = number of vertices
  • E = number of edges
  • F = number of faces (including the outer face)

Key Corollary

For a simple planar graph with V ≥ 3:

E ≤ 3V − 6

This implies every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5. This seemingly small fact is the foundation of every proof of the Four Colour Theorem.

The Five Colour Theorem (Heawood, 1890)

Before the Four Colour Theorem was proven, Percy Heawood proved every planar graph is 5-colourable. The proof is elegant and uses induction:

  1. Every planar graph has a vertex v of degree ≤ 5 (from Euler's formula).
  2. Remove v; colour the remaining graph by induction.
  3. If deg(v) ≤ 4, there is a spare colour for v.
  4. If deg(v) = 5, use a Kempe chain argument to free up a colour.

A Historical Journey

1852

Francis Guthrie's Conjecture

While colouring a map of the counties of England, Francis Guthrie noticed that four colours seemed to suffice. He posed the question to his brother Frederick, who passed it to Augustus De Morgan.

1879

Kempe's "Proof"

Alfred Kempe published what he believed was a complete proof. Though later shown to be flawed, he introduced the crucial concept of Kempe chains — maximal connected subgraphs using exactly two colours — which remains central to every proof of the theorem.

1890

Heawood Finds the Error

Percy Heawood identified the flaw in Kempe's argument: the Kempe chain swapping procedure can interfere with itself. He salvaged the method to prove the Five Colour Theorem instead.

1913

Birkhoff Introduces Reducibility

George Birkhoff developed the concept of reducible configurations — subgraph patterns that cannot appear in a minimal counterexample. This became one of the two pillars of the eventual proof.

1922

Franklin's Partial Result

Philip Franklin proved the Four Colour Theorem holds for all maps with at most 25 regions, extending the verified frontier.

1969

Heesch's Discharging Method

Heinrich Heesch developed the discharging method, the second pillar of the proof. By assigning "charges" to vertices and redistributing them, one can show that certain configurations are unavoidable.

1976

Appel & Haken: The Computer-Assisted Proof

Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved the theorem — the first major theorem requiring computer assistance. They showed that 1,476 configurations form an unavoidable set, and each is reducible. The computer checked each configuration over approximately 1,200 hours of computation.

1997

Robertson, Sanders, Seymour & Thomas

A greatly simplified proof using only 633 configurations and 32 discharging rules (vs. 487 in the original). Computer verification took roughly 3 hours on 1990s hardware.

2005

Gonthier's Formal Verification in Coq

Georges Gonthier produced a fully machine-verified proof using the Coq proof assistant. Every logical step is checked by the computer, making the proof a verifiable certificate that eliminates concerns about programming bugs.

Kempe Chains

What Is a Kempe Chain?

A Kempe chain is a maximal connected subgraph containing only vertices of two specific colours.

Swapping the two colours within a Kempe chain always preserves the validity of the colouring. This operation is the engine behind both the Five Colour Theorem proof and the eventual Four Colour Theorem proof.

Why Kempe's Original Proof Fails

Kempe's 1879 argument for the Four Colour Theorem required performing two Kempe chain swaps when a degree-5 vertex was encountered. The error: the second swap can undo the effect of the first, because the two chains may share vertices. Heawood noticed this subtle interference in 1890.

Key Insight: For five colours, only one swap is ever needed, which is why the Five Colour Theorem proof works. For four colours, we need the full machinery of reducibility and discharging.

The Appel-Haken Proof Structure

Proof by Contradiction

  1. Assume a minimal counterexample G exists — the smallest planar graph that is not 4-colourable.
  2. Discharging shows G must contain one of ~1,500 specific configurations.
  3. Reducibility shows each such configuration cannot appear in a minimal counterexample.
  4. Contradiction: G cannot exist. Every planar graph is 4-colourable.

1. Discharging

Assign an initial "charge" to each vertex:

charge(v) = 6 − deg(v)

By Euler's formula, the total charge is always positive (equals 12 for maximal planar graphs). Discharging rules redistribute charge without changing the total. After redistribution, any vertex still holding positive charge must contain a specific configuration from the unavoidable set.

2. Unavoidable Sets

An unavoidable set is a collection of configurations such that every planar graph must contain at least one.

The discharging argument constructs such a set. Appel-Haken used 1,476 configurations; RSST later reduced this to 633.

3. Reducibility

A configuration is reducible if any graph containing it cannot be a minimal counterexample. This is checked by verifying that any 4-colouring of the boundary ring can be extended to the interior.

For a ring of size k, up to 4k colourings must be checked — this is where the computer is essential.
