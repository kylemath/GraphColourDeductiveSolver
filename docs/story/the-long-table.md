# The Long Table

*A play in one long night, for three voices and a café*

---

## Dramatis Personae

**BUCKY**: Mid-forties. Computer scientist who spent ten years on verified compilers, then moved to the formal-methods group of a large lab. Keeps a fountain pen and a mechanical keyboard in the same bag. Believes anything worth saying can be type-checked. Is privately afraid that this belief has made him a worse listener.

**KAMPER**: Early sixties. Pure mathematician in combinatorics and topology, emerita in all but title. Has read Kempe's 1879 paper in the original journal binding and Heawood's 1890 reply more times than she will admit. Distrusts the word "obviously." Distrusts the word "almost" even more.

**APPKEN**: Late thirties. Former physics PhD who became a quantitative analyst. Has run derivatives desks, risk models, and one notorious backtest that turned out to be right. Thinks in payoffs, hedges, and what happens in the tail. Talks fastest when he is least sure.

**THEO**: The evening closer at the café. Twenty-two. Studying for a statistics exam he keeps not studying for.

**ROSA**: The morning baker. Fifty. Has a key, a radio, and no patience for anyone still sitting at a table when the dough needs the counter.

*Offstage, never seen: three colleagues of our trio, each said to be working on the same old problem. Over the evening they are called "the Lean one," "the counting one," and "the replay one." By morning these names will have stopped mattering.*

---

## The Set

*A café called **The Gamut**, inside a converted print shop. The ceiling is high and the original iron press still stands in one corner, painted black and roped off like a museum piece. A long communal table of reclaimed oak runs down the centre. Upstage left is a smaller round table under a hanging brass lamp, half-hidden behind a tall shelf of bread baskets and jars of coffee beans. This is where the play happens.*

*The floor is the café's pride: hexagonal and pentagonal tiles in four colours, terracotta red, slate blue, mustard yellow and bottle green. They are laid in a pattern a local artist called "a map of nowhere." Near the till, if you look closely, two red tiles share an edge.*

*A large antique county map hangs in the front window, its counties hand-tinted in four soft watercolours. A chalkboard menu reads: TODAY: CARDAMOM BUN. SOUP: ROASTED SQUASH. WIFI: ask Theo.*

*Light moves through the night as a running clock. At rise it is early-October afternoon gold, then blue dusk, then sodium-orange streetlight through the window, then darkness lit only by the brass lamp, and finally the grey-violet of the hour before sunrise.*

## Prologue: The Floor

*4:40 p.m. Gold light. The café is busy: laptops, a toddler, two students arguing over a problem set. THEO is behind the counter, his statistics textbook face-down beside the register.*

*KAMPER enters first, unwinding a long scarf. She does not look for a table. She looks at the floor. She stops near the till and crouches, knees protesting, staring at the two red tiles sharing an edge.*

**THEO**: You okay, ma'am?

**KAMPER**: *(not looking up)* Your floor isn't properly coloured.

**THEO**: It's... tile?

**KAMPER**: Two reds touching. There. Right where everyone's feet go. *(She stands.)* It's fine. It's a very human mistake. People have been making it for a hundred and fifty years.

> *Kamper, inside:* Every time. Every café, every bathroom, every cathedral floor. I can't walk on a tiling without auditing it. Forty years of this and still no cure.

*She orders a black coffee and a cardamom bun and takes the round table under the brass lamp, the one behind the bread shelf. It is the table you choose when you want to be found only by the people you're expecting.*

*BUCKY enters, already talking into an earbud.*

**BUCKY**: *(into the earbud)* No, the build passed. The build passed. I'm saying a passing build isn't a reason to... yes. Yes. I'll look tonight. *(He taps the earbud off, sees KAMPER, and lights up.)* Kamper.

**KAMPER**: Bucky. You look like a man who's been told a build passed.

**BUCKY**: You heard that.

**KAMPER**: The whole room heard it. The toddler is now sceptical of continuous integration.

*He orders a flat white and sits. A moment later APPKEN arrives, overcoat over a hoodie, phone in hand, scrolling as he walks. He nearly collides with the iron press.*

**APPKEN**: Sorry. Sorry. *(to the press)* Sorry. *(He sees the others.)* Oh good, you're both here. I thought this might be the wrong Gamut.

**BUCKY**: There's more than one?

**APPKEN**: There's a Gamut Wellness in the strip mall. I went there first. Someone tried to sell me a crystal.

**KAMPER**: Did you buy it?

**APPKEN**: I hedged. I bought a smaller crystal.

*He orders a double espresso and a soup and sits. The brass lamp makes a warm circle around the three of them. Outside the light is beginning to slant.*

## Scene One: Water-Cooler Fragments

*5:00 p.m. The gold has deepened. The café hums.*

**APPKEN**: So. Who goes first.

**BUCKY**: Goes first with what?

**APPKEN**: Oh, come on. You both texted me "need to talk, not on Slack." Nobody says "not on Slack" unless it's either a resignation or gossip.

**KAMPER**: Or both.

**APPKEN**: Or both. Which is it?

*A pause. Each of them waits for another to lay down a card.*

> *Bucky, inside:* If I say it first and I'm wrong, I'm the guy who got excited about a rumour. If I say it first and I'm right, I'm the guy who spread it. Either way I'd rather someone else went first.

> *Kamper, inside:* They're both waiting for me. Fine. I'm old. I'm allowed to be the fool.

**KAMPER**: I heard something about the Four Colour problem.

*APPKEN puts his phone face-down. BUCKY, who had been stirring his flat white, stops.*

**APPKEN**: *(slowly)* So did I.

**BUCKY**: *(a beat)* So did I.

*Silence. Someone at the long table laughs at a video. The espresso machine shrieks.*

**KAMPER**: Well. That's either a coincidence or a conspiracy, and I'm too tired for a conspiracy.

**APPKEN**: Who did you hear it from?

**KAMPER**: Not *from*. *Around*. A seminar coffee break two weeks ago. A young man I'd never met was explaining something to a postdoc, and I heard the phrase "Heawood's boundary." Nobody says "Heawood's boundary" by accident. I edged over by the biscuits and listened for as long as decency allowed.

**BUCKY**: How long does decency allow?

**KAMPER**: Two biscuits. Three if they're small.

**APPKEN**: What did you hear in three small biscuits?

**KAMPER**: *(hesitating)* I want to be careful. I heard parts. They were talking about someone else's work, a colleague, the one who does the counting arguments. I'll call her the counting one. Apparently she has proved, in a proof assistant and compiled, that every spherical map on at most eleven vertices is four-colourable.

*BUCKY nods quickly. APPKEN frowns.*

**APPKEN**: Eleven vertices? That's... I'm sorry, isn't that tiny? Couldn't you just brute-force every graph up to eleven vertices on a laptop?

**KAMPER**: *(pleased he asked)* That's the point. She didn't brute-force it. That's what made me eat the third biscuit. It's a counting argument, not an enumeration.

> *Kamper, inside:* Careful. You only heard the end of the sentence. Reconstruct it, don't invent it.

**KAMPER**: *(cont'd)* As I understood it, she sharpened the Euler-type bound for these maps. Instead of the usual inequality she got one with a "plus two" in a useful place. Edges plus two is at most vertices plus faces. And then...

*She reaches for a paper napkin, takes a pencil from her coat pocket, and writes:*

```
e + 2  ≤  s + f        (s = vertices that actually have edges)
```

**KAMPER**: *(cont'd)* Suppose every vertex has degree at least five. Each edge has two ends, so five times s is at most two e. Every face has at least three sides, so three f is at most two e. Substitute, and...

*She writes quickly. BUCKY leans in. APPKEN leans in further, competitively.*

```
5s ≤ 2e    →  e ≥ 5s/2
3f ≤ 2e    →  f ≤ 2e/3
e + 2 ≤ s + 2e/3   →   s ≥ e/3 + 2  ≥  5s/6 + 2
                   →   s ≥ 12
```

**KAMPER**: *(tapping the napkin)* Twelve. If every vertex has degree at least five, you need at least twelve vertices. So below twelve, there is always a vertex of degree four or less.

**APPKEN**: And degree four or less you can always... handle?

**KAMPER**: That's the other half. That's where Kempe comes in. The *correct* part of Kempe.

**BUCKY**: *(quietly, almost to himself)* Gate A.

*KAMPER and APPKEN both look at him.*

**KAMPER**: What did you say?

**BUCKY**: *(caught)* Gate A. It's... that's what they call it. The degree-four extension. I heard it called Gate A.

> *Bucky, inside:* Well. There goes my card.

**APPKEN**: *Gate* A. Like there's a Gate B.

**BUCKY**: There's a Gate B. The eleven-vertex result is Gate B. There's a C, D, E, F.

**KAMPER**: *(sitting back)* Where did *you* hear this?

**BUCKY**: The Lean hack evening last Thursday. The PlaneMap people. I was there to help a student with a build problem. Mathlib version mismatch, the usual misery. Someone at the next table had a laptop open, and I saw a file called `FourColorExtension.lean`. Compiled. Green check. I nearly fell off my stool.

**APPKEN**: *Four colour.* Compiled.

**BUCKY**: That's what I thought. That's what I thought for about six hours. Then I went home and realised I'd only seen the filename.

**KAMPER**: *(gently)* And the file proves?

**BUCKY**: The theorem in that file is called... `four_color_extension`. Its job is to say that if you have a four-colouring of a spherical map with one vertex deleted, and that vertex has degree at most four, you can extend the colouring to the whole map.

**KAMPER**: That's Kempe's argument. The part he got right.

**BUCKY**: Right. But formally. Spherically. With an actual separation lemma about alternating walks, not a picture. I heard someone call it `alternating_walks_intersect`. You have four neighbours in rotation order. If two opposite pairs are joined by bichromatic chains, the chains have to cross. They can't, so one of them isn't connected, and you swap.

*He draws on a second napkin, faster and less tidily than KAMPER. It shows a square of four dots around a centre and two crossing paths, one of them struck through.*

```
        v1 (red)
          |
v4 (grn)--r--v2 (blue)
          |
        v3 (yel)

red–yellow chain v1 → v3   AND   blue–green chain v2 → v4
           cannot both exist on a sphere.
     swap one  →  a colour opens up for r.
```

**KAMPER**: *(examining it like a grade)* Acceptable.

**BUCKY**: High praise.

**KAMPER**: It is. Do you know what the subtle part is?

**BUCKY**: The subtle part is that it's a *spherical* theorem. It doesn't hold for graphs in general. A graph where every subgraph has a vertex of degree at most four is not automatically four-colourable. K5 is a counterexample: every vertex has degree four and it needs five colours. Somebody wrote that right into the plan, in bold, so nobody would overclaim.

> *Kamper, inside:* Good. Someone on that team has been bitten before. That's the scar tissue you want.

**APPKEN**: *(who has been quiet, eating soup)* Okay. Okay. I have to say something and I'm worried it's going to make me sound like an idiot.

**KAMPER**: That's the only kind of thing worth saying at this table.

**APPKEN**: My piece is different. Mine is about the stuff that *didn't* work.

*He wipes his mouth and pulls his phone back over. He scrolls but doesn't show them the screen.*

**APPKEN**: *(cont'd)* I was at a dinner. Finance people, a couple of ex-academics, someone's partner who does topology. And the topology partner, very drunk on an excellent Barolo, starts telling the table about a friend, the replay one, I'll call him, who does all the computation for this group. Huge amounts of it. And the thing she kept coming back to was that they *kill* things. They have a list of dead conjectures, and they're proud of it.

**BUCKY**: *(nodding)* The navigator tree.

**APPKEN**: The what?

**BUCKY**: They have a website, a proof navigator. It's a tree. Every node is a claim or a sub-goal, and every node has a status: proved, in progress, exploring, blocked, killed. Killed nodes stay in the tree. They don't delete them.

**APPKEN**: *(delighted)* Like a trade blotter where you keep the losing trades on the screen.

**KAMPER**: Like a proper notebook.

**APPKEN**: Right. So, according to my Barolo source, there was a conjecture called "swap sufficiency." Something about only needing swaps among four of five colours...

**KAMPER**: Using only colours one to four, never touching the fifth, when going from five colours to four.

**APPKEN**: That. Dead at nine vertices. Forty-eight counterexamples.

**KAMPER**: *(quietly)* Good. Good that they found them.

**APPKEN**: And something called "surface tension rigidity," which I assumed was a cocktail, also dead. And a whole branch involving topological quantum field theory, which was dead because some symbols had the wrong signs.

**BUCKY**: Six-j symbols.

**APPKEN**: That, yes. Then she said the thing that's been rattling round my head for a week. She said, "And the whole constructive proof is ninety-five percent done."

*A beat. KAMPER puts her cup down very carefully.*

> *Kamper, inside:* There it is. Ninety-five percent. That number has been said about this problem in every decade since 1879. Somewhere a ghost in a frock coat is nodding.

> *Bucky, inside:* Ninety-five percent of what, measured how? Lines of proof? Lemmas? Sorries?

> *Appken, inside:* They both flinched. Why did they both flinch? It's a good number. If a strategy were ninety-five percent built I'd fund it.

**KAMPER**: Ninety-five percent.

**APPKEN**: That's what she said.

**KAMPER**: When was this dinner?

**APPKEN**: Last month. But she was quoting something older. A wrap-up document, I think. Spring.

**KAMPER**: *(carefully)* Then I suspect she was quoting a document that said the remaining five percent was a single lemma. And that the same document added, in the next sentence, that the last five percent might be as hard as the whole theorem.

**APPKEN**: *(slowly)* ...How did you know that?

**KAMPER**: Because anyone honest would have written it.

*A pause. APPKEN laughs, a little embarrassed.*

**APPKEN**: She did say that too. I didn't put it on the blotter.

**BUCKY**: Selective memory.

**APPKEN**: Survivorship bias. It's a professional hazard. The ninety-five survives, the caveat dies.

**KAMPER**: The caveat always dies first. It doesn't fit in a tweet.

*THEO arrives with a refill of water and glances at the napkins.*

**THEO**: Is that... homework?

**KAMPER**: Something like that.

**THEO**: Good luck. *(He goes.)*

> *Theo, inside:* Three grown adults drawing dots on napkins at five o'clock on a Tuesday. I'm going to be like that one day. Or I'm going to fail statistics.

## Scene Two: The Pieces on the Table

*5:45 p.m. Blue dusk has begun. The long table is half-empty. The toddler has gone. The window map has dimmed to grey and yellow.*

**APPKEN**: Okay. I want to organise this. If this were a desk meeting I'd put up a whiteboard. Can we... can somebody list what's real?

**BUCKY**: "Real" meaning compiled?

**KAMPER**: "Real" meaning *true*. Compiled is a subset.

**BUCKY**: Compiled is the subset I can check.

**APPKEN**: Fine. Three columns. *(He takes a fresh napkin.)* Compiled. Computed. Rumoured.

*He draws the columns. This becomes the most important napkin of the night, though none of them knows it yet.*

**BUCKY**: Compiled. One: they rebuilt plane maps inside Lean on top of Mathlib. Retooled it. There's a structure called `SphericalMap`: a finite simple graph, a cyclic rotation at each vertex, and a "filling" property.

**APPKEN**: What's filling?

**BUCKY**: *(hesitating)* I... think it's the condition that makes it a sphere and not some other surface. Something about even sets of edges bounding regions.

**KAMPER**: Mod-two cycles bound. Every even edge-combination is a boundary of faces. That's what makes a sphere a sphere combinatorially: no handles for a cycle to wrap around.

**BUCKY**: Yes. That. And the important part is that they don't assume planarity as an axiom. They *construct* these maps, and they *prove* the filling property for the constructed ones. No oracle.

> *Bucky, inside:* That's the part that impressed me most, and I can't make either of them feel it. Not assuming the planar oracle. Ten years ago half the formalisations I reviewed hid their hardest lemma in an axiom and called it a "standard fact."

**APPKEN**: Two?

**BUCKY**: Two: the Five Colour Theorem compiles. For spherical maps, for their generated plane maps. And I heard the axiom audit comes back with just the three standard ones: propositional extensionality, choice, quotient soundness. Nothing extra smuggled in.

**KAMPER**: Kempe's theorem, with Heawood's correction, done right.

**BUCKY**: Done right. Three: Gate A, degree-four extension, compiled. Four: Gate B, the eleven-vertex result, compiled. The rank bound, elimination certificates, all of it. There's even a certificate whose length is bounded by the number of edges.

**APPKEN**: *(writing)* Five?

**BUCKY**: Five is a small thing, but I like it. An *executable* Kempe swap. Real code inside the proof assistant: find the component, swap the colours, and a proof that it equals the mathematical notion of reachability and preserves properness. With a cost accounting: n cubed adjacency checks for n rounds.

**KAMPER**: Why do you like that?

**BUCKY**: Because the whole point, the way I heard it, isn't just to prove colourings exist. The finish line is an *algorithm*. A polynomial-time four-colouring algorithm, proved correct inside Lean, that you can actually run. Then they race it against DSATUR, against SAT solvers, against genetic algorithms and random search.

*APPKEN stops writing.*

**APPKEN**: Wait. They want to *benchmark* it?

**BUCKY**: That's the finish line as I heard it. Not "a proof exists." A proof that *gives you the colouring*, step by step, with a proof that it terminates in polynomially many steps. Then actually benchmark it.

**APPKEN**: *(grinning)* That's a backtest. They want a proof with a backtest.

**KAMPER**: *(dry)* Appel and Haken had a backtest. It took twelve hundred hours on a mainframe.

**APPKEN**: Different backtest. They backtested the *proof*. This one is backtesting the *colouring*.

> *Kamper, inside:* He's right, and it's a distinction I hadn't let myself make. Appel–Haken, Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas, Gonthier: all of them say "the configurations are reducible, and here is a machine that checks each one." None of them says "here is a rule you could teach a child, and the rule always finishes." That's what I miss when I teach it. You can't explain the 1976 proof to a sixteen-year-old. You can only explain what it *checks*.

**APPKEN**: Okay. Compiled column: five things. Computed column. That's mine, mostly. *(He looks at his phone.)* The replay one. The killed conjectures I've given you. Then the one that's been eating my week.

**BUCKY**: Kittell.

*APPKEN looks up sharply.*

**APPKEN**: You heard about Kittell?

**BUCKY**: I heard the word. I didn't know what it meant. Someone at the hack night said, "Did it survive Kittell?" the way you'd ask if a startup survived a recession.

**KAMPER**: Kittell's graph. Twenty-three vertices. It's a famous one: a planar graph built to show that a certain naive Kempe-swap strategy can loop forever.

**APPKEN**: *(fast now)* Right. And the replay one did, from scratch, an independent replay: took the graph, checked planarity, and for each degree-five vertex (there are fifteen) deleted it, enumerated *every* proper four-colouring of what was left, up to renaming colours, and then built the whole graph of Kempe swaps between those colourings.

**BUCKY**: Every colouring?

**APPKEN**: Six thousand three hundred and fifty root-colouring states across the fifteen roots. And the good news first, which I'm told is unusual for this group...

**KAMPER**: *(smiling despite herself)* Go on.

**APPKEN**: Every root's swap graph was connected. And from *every* state, you can reach a colouring where the five neighbours of the deleted vertex use only three colours. That means a colour is free and you can put the vertex back. And in at most *four* moves. Every single one.

*A pause.*

**BUCKY**: That's... that's good.

**APPKEN**: That's good. Now the bad news. Which, honestly, is the interesting part.

> *Appken, inside:* This is the part I've been trying to explain to my wife for a week. She nodded at the right places, but I could see her pricing my sanity.

**APPKEN**: *(cont'd)* Suppose you want a *rule*. Not a search, a rule. "When you see this, do that." The natural thing is to look only at the boundary: the five neighbours, their colours, and for each pair of colours, which neighbours are connected to which by chains of that pair. Six colour pairs, so six partitions of the five neighbours. That's your signature. They called it sigma.

**KAMPER**: The boundary information. What Kempe himself looked at.

**APPKEN**: Right. Across all fifteen roots there are forty distinct sigmas. Forty situations. And here's the first problem: two concrete colourings with the *same* sigma can have *different* sets of next sigmas. Sigma isn't a complete description. Two people can look at the same screen and be in different markets.

**BUCKY**: The abstraction isn't a bisimulation.

**APPKEN**: I don't know what that word means, but I believe you.

**BUCKY**: It means the coarse view doesn't predict the fine view's next step.

**APPKEN**: Then yes, exactly that. So they played a game. It's a robust game. You must choose *one* boundary action, a colour pair and which boundary vertices you swap through, that works for *every* concrete colouring hiding behind that sigma. Then they did backward induction. Start from the target signatures, the winners. Add any signature that has an action all of whose possible results are already winners. Repeat until nothing changes.

**KAMPER**: The attractor.

**APPKEN**: The robust attractor. It captures thirty-five of the forty. Five lose. Five sigmas from which no single boundary-only rule is guaranteed to work.

*He writes it in the "Computed" column:*

```
KITTELL (23 v, 63 e, 15 degree-5 roots)
  6,350 states; every one reaches target in ≤ 4 swaps   ✓
  boundary signature σ: 40 signatures
  robust boundary game: 35 win / 5 LOSE
```

**APPKEN**: *(cont'd)* So: the colourings are fine, but the *rule* that only looks at the boundary isn't. And I want to be careful, because she was very careful at the dinner even drunk: it doesn't say the theorem is false. It says *that kind of rule* can't be the whole story.

**KAMPER**: That kind of rule is Kempe's kind of rule. That's 1879 failing again, in a new costume.

**APPKEN**: It gets better. If you *don't pool* the roots, if you play the game separately for each root, eleven of the fifteen roots have zero losing signatures. Perfect. Four roots still lose five each. Roots three, thirteen, seventeen, twenty-one.

**BUCKY**: So *which root* you choose matters.

**APPKEN**: Which root you choose matters.

*They sit with that. The light outside has gone fully blue. THEO switches on the overhead pendants. The brass lamp is now one warm circle among several.*

**KAMPER**: *(after a while)* And the "Rumoured" column?

**APPKEN**: *(looking at it, empty)* I was going to put "ninety-five percent."

**KAMPER**: Don't.

**APPKEN**: *(crossing out a pencil mark that isn't there)* I won't.

## Scene Three: The Dawning

*6:40 p.m. Full dark outside. The long table is mostly empty now: one student asleep on her textbook, a couple sharing a slice of cake. The window map is lit only by the streetlamp, its four watercolours turned to four greys.*

*BUCKY has his laptop out but hasn't opened it. KAMPER is turning the "Compiled / Computed" napkin around with one finger.*

**KAMPER**: Let me say back what I think we have, and you tell me where I'm lying.

**APPKEN**: Deal.

**KAMPER**: Degree four or less, compiled. Fewer than twelve vertices, compiled. Five colours, compiled, the honest way. So any counterexample to the Four Colour Theorem, any smallest one, would have to be a sphere where every vertex has degree at least five, with at least twelve vertices.

**BUCKY**: Right.

**KAMPER**: That's not new. Heawood knew that in 1890. That's the boundary that broke Kempe. It's where I'd expect the boundary to be.

**BUCKY**: *(quietly)* "Heawood's boundary."

**KAMPER**: Which is the phrase that made me eat three biscuits. Because the young man said it in a particular way. Not "we're stuck at Heawood's boundary," but "the *verified* advance reaches Heawood's boundary." As in: everything up to here is now machine-checked, with no gaps, by people who wrote down what they *didn't* know. And the next step is new mathematics.

**APPKEN**: So the ninety-five percent...

**KAMPER**: Is a matter of what you're counting. If you count the infrastructure, the build, the definitions, the lemmas, maybe it's ninety-five. If you count the *idea*, you've got everything Kempe and Heawood had, rebuilt to modern standards, and the cliff is exactly where it was.

> *Appken, inside:* That's a sobering re-mark. My position is the same but my valuation just dropped forty percent. No, it didn't drop. I mis-marked it in the first place.

**BUCKY**: But it's not exactly where it was.

*KAMPER raises an eyebrow.*

**BUCKY**: *(cont'd, opening his laptop)* I mean, the cliff is the same height. But Kempe and Heawood didn't know *what shape* the next step had to be. Here, I think they've written down the shape. That's what Gate D is.

**APPKEN**: Okay, so what's Gate D? You keep saying gates like I'm supposed to know the floor plan.

**BUCKY**: I only saw the table of gates on someone's screen. Let me try to recall it honestly. *(He types briefly, then stops himself.)* No, I'm not going to look it up. I'll say what I remember and you two correct me.

**KAMPER**: That's the spirit of the evening.

**BUCKY**: A is degree four. B is the twelve-vertex thing. C is "boundary semantics": defining exactly what information you're allowed to look at around one candidate configuration, and what moves are legal. D is the hard one: a *uniform* reduction with a *progress measure*. E is "general theorem, plus executable, plus polynomial." F is getting from ordinary planar graphs, the kind you'd draw, to their spherical maps.

**APPKEN**: And D is the gap.

**BUCKY**: D is the gap. I remember the line: "this is the mathematical four-colour gap." It said that in the table.

*KAMPER pulls the napkin back and turns it over. On the clean side she writes, slowly:*

```
GATE D
  T  : spherical triangulation, every degree ≥ 5  (so ≥ 12 vertices)
  r  : a degree-5 vertex we choose  ("root")
  H  = T − r ;  r's five neighbours B, in cyclic order
  c  : a proper 4-colouring of H
  move: pick 2 colours, pick ONE connected component of that 2-colour
        subgraph, swap the colours on that component  (a Kempe swap)
  target: B uses ≤ 3 colours  →  r gets the missing colour
```

**KAMPER**: Is that it?

**APPKEN**: That's the game they replayed on Kittell. Yes.

**KAMPER**: And what do they want to *prove* about it?

**BUCKY**: This is the part I remember most clearly because it gave me chills. It's quantified. There are fixed constants, C and k, and two procedures. *Select* looks at T and picks a root. *Step* looks at the current state and makes a move. And for *every* triangulation in the class, and for *every* four-colouring of T minus the selected root, Step reaches a target in at most C times n to the k moves.

*He writes it under KAMPER's block.*

```
∃ C, k, Select, Step   (all polynomial-time)
∀ T  (min degree 5 triangulation)
   r := Select(T)
   ∀ proper 4-colouring c of T − r
       Step reaches a target in ≤ C·|V(T)|^k Kempe swaps
```

*A long silence. APPKEN reads it twice.*

**APPKEN**: That's stronger than the theorem.

**KAMPER**: *(pleased)* Yes.

**APPKEN**: That's a lot stronger than the theorem. The theorem just needs *one* good colouring. This says every colouring of the smaller graph can be walked to a good one, quickly, by a rule.

**BUCKY**: They wrote that too. "A root-selection strengthening." They're clear that if it fails, the Four Colour Theorem doesn't fail. Only this route does. Then they'd fall back to a weaker version.

**KAMPER**: Which is?

**BUCKY**: Something about certificates. Instead of "every colouring works," the recursion hands you a colouring *plus a certificate* that it's in the winning region. The certificate has to be *preserved* by the recursion, and its existence has to be *proved*, not assumed. They had a line about circularity: if your invariant is just "this colouring is extendible," you've smuggled the theorem in as a premise.

**KAMPER**: *(nodding slowly)* Yes. That's the trap. That's always the trap.

**APPKEN**: And the "progress measure" part?

## Scene Four: Soup, and Everything After

### i. The Price of a Colouring

*7:30 p.m. Three bowls of roasted squash soup steam on the round table. The napkins have been weighted down with sugar sachets so the soup doesn't land on Gate D. Outside, a tram passes and its light slides across the window map.*

**APPKEN**: *(spoon in the air)* Okay, here's my first thing, and I want you to tell me why it's stupid.

**KAMPER**: Gladly.

**APPKEN**: The robust game they played on Kittell, with backward induction from the targets. Layer zero is the winners. Layer one is anything that has a move guaranteed to land in layer zero. Layer two, and so on.

**BUCKY**: The attractor.

**APPKEN**: That's an options lattice. That's exactly how you price an American option. You start at expiry, where the payoff is known, and you walk backwards. At each node you ask whether the best thing you can do is exercise or wait. The value at each node is the value of the best action. "Layer j" *is* a price. It's the number of moves to guaranteed exercise.

**KAMPER**: And that's the distance to the target. Which is circular.

**APPKEN**: Which is circular *if you compute it by walking the lattice*. Yes. Yes. Exactly. That's the thing that hit me during the soup.

*He puts the spoon down.*

**APPKEN**: *(cont'd)* Nobody on a desk prices an option by simulating every path of the world. You can't. You need a *formula*. A closed form, or something cheap that's guaranteed to agree with the lattice where it matters. Black–Scholes isn't "simulate every price path," it's "here's a function of five numbers you can see right now." The trick that makes it work is finding the right *state variables*. The handful of observable quantities the price depends on.

**BUCKY**: So you're saying: don't compute the rank by search. Find a potential that's cheap to compute and *provably* goes down.

**APPKEN**: A supermartingale. Well, there's no randomness, so...

**KAMPER**: A Lyapunov function. A potential.

**APPKEN**: A potential. A number you can compute from the state in polynomial time, which is zero at the targets, and for which, from every non-target state, *some* legal Kempe swap makes it strictly smaller. And it can't be secretly "distance to target." It has to come from something you can see.

**KAMPER**: *(slowly)* The p and q.

**APPKEN**: The p and q. Exactly. That's what they're asking for. They wrote down the shape of the pricing formula, and they're waiting for someone to find the state variables.

> *Kamper, inside:* He's not wrong. It's an old idea in a new suit, but the suit matters. Mathematicians say "find a monovariant" and students stare at us. He says "price it," and I can see the whole structure: what's observable, what's the payoff, what's the hedge.

**BUCKY**: Here's why it's not stupid. Here's why it's *testable*. Tonight, if we wanted. We have, well, the replay one has, all 6,350 states for Kittell. Every state, every move. Any candidate potential you write down, you can check in seconds: does every non-target state have at least one move that makes it go down?

**APPKEN**: A backtest.

**BUCKY**: A backtest. Which kills bad potentials fast. It can't prove a good one, because a potential that survives Kittell might die on the next graph. But it's a cheap kill.

**KAMPER**: Like the boundary signature.

**BUCKY**: Exactly like the signature. Sigma was *already* a candidate state variable. Kittell killed it. Five losing signatures.

*APPKEN goes very still.*

**APPKEN**: Say that again.

**BUCKY**: Sigma was a candidate state variable, and Kittell killed it.

**APPKEN**: No. The *way* it died. Two colourings with the same sigma had different futures.

**BUCKY**: Same signature, different successor sets. Yes.

**APPKEN**: *(standing up, which startles THEO across the room)* That's an *incomplete market*.

**KAMPER**: Sit down.

**APPKEN**: *(sitting)* Sorry. That's an incomplete market. In finance, if two states of the world look identical in every price you can observe, but they pay off differently, you *can't hedge*. There's risk you can't see. The fix is never "try harder with the same instruments." The fix is to *add an instrument*. You find the security whose price differs between those two states, and you add it to what you observe. Then the market is complete and the hedge exists.

**BUCKY**: *(laughing)* That's CEGAR.

**APPKEN**: That's what?

**BUCKY**: Counterexample-guided abstraction refinement. It's what model checkers do. You've got a huge state space and you abstract it: you only look at some features. You try to prove the property on the abstraction. If it fails, you look at *why*: you find the spurious counterexample, a place where two concrete states got merged that shouldn't have been. Then you refine the abstraction with *just enough* new information to split them. Repeat.

**APPKEN**: *(pointing at him with a spoon)* That's adding an instrument.

**BUCKY**: That's adding an instrument!

**KAMPER**: *(dry)* You've each just discovered that you work in the same field.

**BUCKY**: But it's actually concrete. Here's what you do. Kittell gives you collisions: specific pairs of concrete colourings with the same sigma and different futures. For each collision, you ask what *cheapest* extra piece of information tells them apart. Something about the exterior, not just the boundary. Something about how the chains are nested. Something about what happens two moves ahead. You add exactly that, recompute the robust game, and see if the five losers become winners.

**KAMPER**: And you'll find that it works.

**BUCKY**: *(surprised)* Isn't that good?

**KAMPER**: You'll find that it works on Kittell, because Kittell is one finite graph and you can always refine until every state is distinguished. If you refine all the way, sigma *is* the full colouring and the game is trivially won. You'll have built a lookup table and called it a rule.

*BUCKY deflates a little.*

> *Bucky, inside:* She's right. That's the failure mode. You overfit the abstraction to the fixture until the abstraction is the fixture.

**APPKEN**: That's overfitting. That's literally the backtest problem. You torture the data until it confesses.

**KAMPER**: So the refinement has to stay *bounded*. It has to be a fixed amount of extra information, no matter how large the triangulation gets. That's the real test, not Kittell.

**BUCKY**: Okay. Okay. So the question is: is there any *bounded* extra information that's likely to be enough? Something with a structural reason to be small?

*KAMPER pulls the crossing-chains napkin towards her. She looks at it for a long time.*

**KAMPER**: Yes. Maybe. There's a reason it might be small.

*BUCKY and APPKEN wait.*

**KAMPER**: *(cont'd)* Chains of disjoint colour pairs can't cross. That's the whole engine of the degree-four argument. Red–yellow and blue–green can't cross on a sphere. So around a boundary of five vertices, the way the chains connect them isn't arbitrary. It's *non-crossing*. Planar. Nested, like parentheses.

**BUCKY**: Non-crossing partitions.

**KAMPER**: Non-crossing partitions. Which would make it a tiny menu, a few dozen shapes at most, and... *(She stops.)* No. Wait. I'm cheating.

**BUCKY**: Where?

**KAMPER**: *Disjoint* pairs. Red–yellow can't cross blue–green. But look at the boundary we care about: five neighbours, four colours, so one colour repeats. The chains that matter there are things like red–yellow and red–blue. They *share* red. Chains that share a colour share vertices. They can touch. They can cross. The topology that fences in Kempe's degree-four case doesn't fence these in.

**APPKEN**: So no tiny menu.

**KAMPER**: Not from that argument. Counting what *would* fit on a menu proves nothing. Whatever extra information we add, someone has to define it, including for chains that share a colour, and then prove it's enough.

**APPKEN**: So sigma might be missing something, but we don't know what.

**KAMPER**: Exactly that. I'd still like to see whether some nesting data repairs the five losing Kittell signatures. As an experiment, not a promise. If it doesn't, it's dead, and we learn which *other* thing to try.

**BUCKY**: *(already writing)* Refinement candidate one: sigma plus some relative nesting of bichromatic chain-curves around the root. First: define it for chains that share a colour. Test: robust game on Kittell, pooled and per-root. Kill condition: any losing signature remains.

**APPKEN**: You write like a lab notebook.

**BUCKY**: I write like someone who's been burned by a lab notebook.

### ii. Choosing the Strike

**APPKEN**: Okay, the roots. I keep thinking about the roots.

**KAMPER**: Eleven good, four bad.

**APPKEN**: Eleven good, four bad. If I hold fifteen options and I only need one to pay off, that's a portfolio. You don't need every root to work. You need *one*. Hedging across roots. Whatever colouring you're handed, you pick the root that's in the money.

**KAMPER**: You can't.

**APPKEN**: Why not?

**KAMPER**: Look at the quantifiers. *(She taps the Gate D napkin.)* Select looks at T, only at T. Then it picks r. *Then* you're handed a colouring of T minus r. The colouring comes from recursion on a smaller graph, which only exists *after* you've removed r.

*APPKEN reads it. His face falls, then recovers.*

**APPKEN**: I have to choose my strike before the market opens.

**KAMPER**: You have to choose your strike before the market opens.

**APPKEN**: *(quietly)* Huh. That's... that's actually a harder trade. Fine. Fine. Then the root has to be selected *structurally*. From the shape of T alone.

**BUCKY**: Couldn't you cheat? Delete r, recurse, get c. If r is bad for c, then use c to recolour... no. You'd need a colouring of T minus some *other* root r′, and c is a colouring of the wrong graph.

**KAMPER**: You could restrict c to T minus r minus r′ and put r back... but then you're asking for a two-vertex extension, which is a different game with a different target. It's not crazy. But it's a different candidate, and it needs its own invariant. Write it on the "later" napkin.

**BUCKY**: *(writing)* "Later: two-root re-selection after recursion. Needs own target definition and invariant. Don't conflate with Select(T)."

**KAMPER**: So, structurally. From T alone. How do you pick a root in a minimum-degree-five triangulation?

*She smiles. It's the smile of someone who has been waiting forty years for this question at a café.*

**KAMPER**: *(cont'd)* You discharge.

**APPKEN**: I don't know what that means, and I suspect it's not what it means in my world.

**KAMPER**: In your world it means you've lost your job. In mine it's the oldest trick in the book. In a triangulation, give every vertex a charge of six minus its degree. Euler's formula says the total charge is exactly twelve. Always. Positive.

*She writes:*

```
charge(v) = 6 − deg(v)
Σ charge  = 12    (every spherical triangulation)
```

**KAMPER**: *(cont'd)* Degree-five vertices are positive, plus one each. Degree six is zero. Seven and higher are negative. So in a min-degree-five triangulation, the positive charge lives *only* on degree-five vertices. Then you write rules that move charge around, from fives to their neighbours or along paths, without creating or destroying any. Since the total is twelve, at the end *somewhere* still has positive charge. And the rules are designed so that any vertex left with positive charge must have a particular local neighbourhood.

**BUCKY**: That's how the 1976 proof found its unavoidable set.

**KAMPER**: And Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas. Discharging tells you that one of 633 configurations must appear. Then the computer checks that each one is reducible. Discharging is the "somewhere"; reducibility is the "and then."

**APPKEN**: So you want to use discharging for the "somewhere" part, but replace the six hundred and thirty-three "and then"s with...

**KAMPER**: With one rule. Step. The Kempe escape. We don't need 633 configurations that are each reducible by their own bespoke computation. We need discharging to put positive charge on *some* degree-five vertex whose neighbourhood is good enough for *one uniform Step* to work.

> *Kamper, inside:* If this works it's beautiful. If it doesn't, it's beautiful for about a week. That's the usual ratio.

**BUCKY**: So the unavoidable set becomes... "good roots."

**KAMPER**: The unavoidable set becomes a short list of local *root types*, and maybe a very short one. If you're lucky, one sentence: "the selected root is a degree-five vertex with at most this many degree-five neighbours," or "a degree-five vertex adjacent to a degree-six vertex," or whatever the charge dictates.

**APPKEN**: And you'd test it the same way.

**KAMPER**: The same way. Here's the first thing I want to know when I get home. On Kittell, roots three, thirteen, seventeen and twenty-one were bad. Do they look *different*, locally? Their neighbours' degrees, their second neighbourhood. If the four bad roots share a local pattern the eleven good ones don't, then a charge rule that never lands on that pattern is a candidate Select.

**BUCKY**: And if they don't look different?

**KAMPER**: Then the badness isn't local, and either Step needs more exterior information, or the rule has to be bolder. Also interesting.

**APPKEN**: *(writing on his own napkin now, a new one)* Wait. Wait wait wait.

*He holds it up. He has written:*

```
p = charge structure (coarse)  ← Kamper

```

*KAMPER and BUCKY look at the napkin.*

**APPKEN**: So the far-away part of the market moves the near-by prices.

**KAMPER**: That's one way to put it.

## Scene Five: The Locked Room

**BUCKY**: Okay. Can I say the thing I've been not saying?

They track the difference.

**APPKEN**: So whatever the three of us invent has to come with its own extraction.

**BUCKY**: Yes. And that's where I think the machine can help in a different way. Not search for proofs: search for *rules*. Train something, a small decision tree or a little SAT-synthesised policy, on all the small cases. Make it tiny. Then read it. Then *prove* it.

**KAMPER**: Rules from data.

**BUCKY**: Rules from data, then proofs of the rules. Like how you'd find a conjecture by staring at a table, but the table has a million rows and the stare is automated. The danger is the same as before. The learned rule has to be *small*, or it's a lookup table in a trench coat.

**APPKEN**: *(laughing)* A lookup table in a trench coat.

**BUCKY**: I've reviewed so many papers that were a lookup table in a trench coat.

**KAMPER**: I've *written* papers that were a lookup table in a trench coat. In 1987. I'm not proud.

> *Bucky, inside:* She's never told anyone that. I can tell. She's told us because it's midnight and we're the only people in the world.

### ii. Two in the Morning

*2:05 a.m. A police car passes outside without stopping; blue light flicks briefly across the ceiling. Nobody looks up. APPKEN has taken off his overcoat and is wearing only the hoodie. KAMPER has put her scarf back on. BUCKY's laptop is open, but he's using it as a lamp to read napkins, not to type.*

**KAMPER**: There's something we haven't talked about and I want to put it on the table so it doesn't ambush us later.

**APPKEN**: Go.

**KAMPER**: Every few years someone tells me the Four Colour Theorem is "really" a statement about something else. Tait: three-colouring the edges of cubic maps. Flows: nowhere-zero four-flows. Chromatic polynomials: the polynomial of a planar triangulation never vanishes at four. Algebraic versions: Alon–Tarsi, or the vector cross-product formulation. All true. All lovely. Their navigator has nodes for most of them.

**BUCKY**: But.

**KAMPER**: But a reformulation is a translation, not a proof. It moves the difficulty to a different room. It never makes it smaller. The plan says so in so many words: none of those reformulations supply the missing structural theorem.

**APPKEN**: So why bring it up?

**KAMPER**: Because at two in the morning one of us is going to say "but what if we did it with flows" and I want to have already said: fine, but only if it gives us a *potential*. A translation is only worth its weight if the new room has a better ruler in it.

**APPKEN**: A change of numéraire.

**KAMPER**: I have no idea what that is.

**APPKEN**: When a price is hard to compute in dollars, you price it in units of some other asset where the maths is clean. The value doesn't change, but the formula gets simple. That's what a good reformulation does. It changes the numéraire.

**KAMPER**: *(nodding slowly)* Then I'll allow it. Change of numéraire, *only* if the new unit gives us p or q.

**BUCKY**: Write that on the "later" napkin.

**APPKEN**: The later napkin is full.

**BUCKY**: Use the menu.

*APPKEN writes on the back of the menu card: "Tait/flow reformulation: ONLY IF it gives a potential. Change of numéraire."*

### iii. Three in the Morning

*3:20 a.m. The quietest point of the night. The fridge hums in the back. KAMPER has her eyes closed but is not asleep. BUCKY is eating the cardamom bun, which has gone stale, without noticing. APPKEN is staring at the window map.*

**APPKEN**: Can I tell you something embarrassing?

**BUCKY**: It's three in the morning. Everything's embarrassing.

**APPKEN**: I left physics because I wanted something with an answer. Markets have answers. Every evening at five there's a number, and you were right or you weren't. And it's been twelve years of answers, and none of them...

*He stops.*

**KAMPER**: *(eyes still closed)* None of them were *true*.

**APPKEN**: None of them were true. They were *correct*. That's not the same.

**BUCKY**: *(quietly)* I know.

**APPKEN**: Tonight's the first time in twelve years I've thought about something that would still be true after the market closes.

> *Appken, inside:* That's a very stupid thing to say to two people I've known for four years only from conferences and email. I'd say it again.

**KAMPER**: The market's closed now.

**APPKEN**: *(looking around, seeing the dark café for the first time)* ...Huh. When did that happen?

**BUCKY**: *(also looking around)* Where's Theo?

*Pause. All three take in the upturned chairs, the dark counter, the silence.*

**KAMPER**: I believe Theo has gone home.

**APPKEN**: Are we locked in?

**BUCKY**: *(standing, walking to the front door, trying it)* It opens from the inside. It just locks behind you.

**APPKEN**: So we're not locked in.

**BUCKY**: We're not locked in. We're just... still here.

*He comes back. Sits. A long beat. Nobody moves to go.*

**KAMPER**: Well. If we're not locked in, there's no hurry.

*BUCKY laughs, the tired laugh of someone who's past being tired. APPKEN puts his head down on his arms for a moment, then lifts it again.*

**APPKEN**: Okay. Then let's write down the plan.

### iv. The Plan

*4:30 a.m. The menu card, flipped to a fresh section, has become the master document. BUCKY writes; the others dictate. The handwriting deteriorates as the list goes on.*

**BUCKY**: Rules for the plan. Theirs, basically. Each proposal must name: graph class, move relation, invariant, measure, input colouring family, coverage statement, and a falsifying witness. I'm stealing that. It's the best checklist I've heard in years.

**KAMPER**: Steal it. Credit it.

**BUCKY**: Credit it. Okay. Three threads.

*He writes. KAMPER and APPKEN read over his shoulders, so the stage picture is three heads bent into one circle of light.*

```
THREAD K  (Kamper)  — SELECT
  Discharging from charge 6−deg, total 12.
  Goal: a short list of root types such that charge always
        lands on one, AND those types are good for Step.
  Tonight's first test: compare local neighbourhoods of
        Kittell roots 3,13,17,21 (bad) vs the 11 good roots.
  Kill: bad and good roots locally indistinguishable.

THREAD B  (Bucky)   — WHAT STEP SEES
  Refine σ with BOUNDED extra info: nesting order of
        bichromatic chain-curves around the root (must be
        DEFINED for chains sharing a colour; no Catalan
        shortcut).  Then maybe 2-step interaction bits.
  Test: robust boundary game on Kittell, pooled and per-root.
  Then: icosahedron (Select is useless there — Step alone).
  Kill: losing signatures survive, OR refinement grows with n.

THREAD A  (Appken)  — THE MEASURE (p, q)
  Look for a cheap potential, NOT distance-to-target.
  p from structure (Thread K), q from refined σ (Thread B).
  Backtest: on all Kittell states, does every non-target state
        have a legal single-component swap that lowers (p,q)?
  Plot worst-case distance vs n ("term structure") on small
        min-degree-5 triangulations to shape the search.
  Kill: any non-target state with no decreasing move.

LATER (do not conflate):
  - two-root re-selection after recursion
  - interior (non-boundary) swaps as instruments
  - macro-moves of bounded length, described not enumerated
  - Tait/flow ONLY IF it yields a potential
  - extraction: every "exists a move" must become "here is the move"

NOT ALLOWED:
  - "and simultaneously"
  - distance-to-target as a rank
  - "extendible" as an invariant
  - calling a passing sweep a proof
```

*BUCKY puts down the pen. They look at it.*

**APPKEN**: That's a good term sheet.

**KAMPER**: That's a good *research plan*. It's honest about where it dies.

**BUCKY**: Should we... send this to them? The three of them?

*A pause. The real question of the evening.*

**KAMPER**: Not tonight. Not yet. We send them *results*. Even negative ones. Especially negative ones. A list of ideas from three people at a café is a list of ideas from three people at a café. They have plenty of ideas. What they don't have is three more people who have already *killed* some of them.

**APPKEN**: Bring them a dead conjecture, not a live hunch.

**KAMPER**: Bring them a dead conjecture, properly labelled. That's the most useful gift in mathematics.

> *Bucky, inside:* There it is. The thing I came here to learn without knowing it. Not how to be right. How to hand someone a well-made failure.

### v. Five-Forty

*5:40 a.m. Without anyone noticing, the black rectangle of the window has become very dark blue. The streetlamp outside clicks off on its timer. The window map's four watercolours are faintly, faintly visible again, as shapes if not yet colours.*

*KAMPER is asleep, upright, scarf around her chin. APPKEN is drawing a small triangulated sphere on the last napkin. BUCKY is watching him.*

**BUCKY**: *(whispering)* What's that?

**APPKEN**: *(whispering)* Icosahedron. I've never drawn one. I wanted to see it.

**BUCKY**: Every vertex looks the same.

**APPKEN**: Every vertex looks the same. Nowhere for charge to hide. If our rule works here, it works because Step is good, not because we were clever about where to stand.

**BUCKY**: That's a fair test.

**APPKEN**: It's the fairest test there is. It's the market with no information.

*He writes tiny numbers on the twelve vertices, then colours a few with a pen: R, B, Y, G.*

---

## Scene Six: Rosa

*6:52 a.m. The window is grey-violet and brightening. The four watercolours of the county map can now be told apart. A key turns in the BACK door. A radio, playing an old soul song, enters before anyone does. Then ROSA: flour-dusted apron, canvas bag, hair tied back. She flicks on the kitchen lights, which spill out through the swinging door, sets down her bag, and walks into the front of the café to turn on the ovens' display lights. She stops.*

*Three people at the round table under the brass lamp. One asleep. Two with pens. Eleven napkins, two receipts, a menu card, a stale bun.*

*A long beat.*

**ROSA**: *(not a scream exactly, but close)* ...WHAT.

*KAMPER wakes with a jolt. APPKEN drops his pen. BUCKY stands up so fast his chair tips back against the bread shelf, which shakes; one jar of coffee beans rocks and settles.*

**BUCKY**: Good morning!

**ROSA**: *Good morning?* How did you... we're *closed*. We've been closed since eleven. Did you break in?

**APPKEN**: We were already in.

**ROSA**: That's *worse*.

**KAMPER**: *(rising with dignity, adjusting her scarf)* Madam, I apologise sincerely. The young man, Theo, very kindly told us to let ourselves out. We... failed to.

**ROSA**: For eight *hours*?

**KAMPER**: *(looking at the window, and for the first time seeing the morning)* ...Apparently.

**ROSA**: *(looking at the napkins)* What is all this?

*A beat. The three look at each other.*

**APPKEN**: A term sheet.

**BUCKY**: A research plan.

**KAMPER**: A floor.

*ROSA stares at them. Her radio plays on in the kitchen.*

**ROSA**: Out. All three. I've got forty croissants to laminate and I need this table. *Out.*

*They gather the napkins frantically. KAMPER folds them into her coat pocket with the care of someone handling a manuscript. APPKEN rescues the menu card. BUCKY shuts his laptop and tries to put the chair back down, then realises he should put it up on the table like all the others, then can't decide, and ROSA takes it out of his hands and does it herself.*

*They file towards the front door. KAMPER stops on the floor near the till, at the two red tiles that share an edge.*

**KAMPER**: Oh. Rosa, one more thing...

**ROSA**: *OUT.*

**KAMPER**: Those two red tiles. If you ever retile, it's a single swap. Red and blue, on the little red-and-blue patch that contains the left one. Just that piece. It doesn't reach the other red tile, and the rest of the floor won't notice. I checked last night, while Appken was talking about bonds.

**ROSA**: *(arms folded, flour on her elbows)* I'll tell the owner.

**KAMPER**: Thank you.

> *Rosa, inside:* Every year it's someone. Last year it was a poet who wrote on the bathroom wall. At least these ones only want to fix the floor.

*The front door opens. Cold air. The first bird. The three step out onto the pavement. The door closes and locks behind them.*

---

## Epilogue: Three Roads

*7:02 a.m. The street outside The Gamut. The café sits on a corner where three streets meet at uneven angles: one runs uphill towards the university, one runs downhill towards the river and the financial district, and one runs flat along the tram line towards the old residential blocks. Above the rooftops to the east, a line of bright gold is about to break. The sky is the colour that has no name, between violet and blue and grey.*

*The three stand in the middle of the meeting point, blinking.*

**APPKEN**: *(looking at the three streets)* Degree three.

**KAMPER**: Hm?

**APPKEN**: This corner. Three roads. It's a vertex of degree three. The easy case.

**KAMPER**: *(smiling, exhausted)* Any colour's free.

**BUCKY**: Tait would like it. It's cubic.

*They laugh, too much, the way you laugh at seven in the morning when you haven't slept.*

*A pause. Nobody wants to be the first to go.*

**BUCKY**: So. Email.

**APPKEN**: Email.

**KAMPER**: Today.

**BUCKY**: Today. Results, not hunches. Even if it's "my thing is dead."

**APPKEN**: *Especially* if it's "my thing is dead."

**KAMPER**: And properly labelled: the class, the move, the measure, the witness.

**BUCKY**: The checklist.

**KAMPER**: The checklist.

*BUCKY takes a step towards the uphill road, towards the university and its lab, then turns back.*

**BUCKY**: I'm going to refine sigma. The nesting of the chain-curves around the root, defined properly this time, including the chains that share a colour, nothing else. Then I'll run the robust game on Kittell, pooled and per-root. If those five losers turn into winners and the extra data stays bounded, I'll go straight to the icosahedron. If they don't, I'll tell you exactly which collision survived, and why.

> *Bucky, inside:* I want to be right. No. I want to know. Those aren't the same. Remember they aren't the same.

**BUCKY**: *(cont'd, aloud, to both)* I'm terrified it'll work on the first try. Then I won't trust it.

**APPKEN**: That's the right thing to be terrified of.

*BUCKY nods, turns, and walks uphill. He walks fast, then faster, laptop bag bumping against his hip. Near the top he takes out his phone and starts typing, and stops walking to type, and starts again.*

*APPKEN turns towards the downhill road, towards the river and the glass towers, then hesitates.*

**APPKEN**: I'm going to price it. The pair, p and q. Not distance, nothing circular, something I could compute from what's on the board. I'll take every Kittell state and check whether every non-target state has a swap that brings the price down. And I'm going to plot the term structure: worst-case moves against size, for every small triangulation where every vertex has degree at least five. If the curve is flat, I'll know what kind of formula to look for.

**KAMPER**: And if it's not flat?

**APPKEN**: Then I'll know what kind of formula *not* to look for. That's worth just as much. Half of finance is knowing which models to never use again.

> *Appken, inside:* I'm going to call in sick. I've never called in sick. I'm going to call in sick and price a colouring.

**APPKEN**: *(cont'd)* I'm so nervous I feel like I'm about to go on a trading floor for the first time. That's... that's a good feeling. I'd forgotten it was a good feeling.

*He walks downhill, overcoat over one arm, and doesn't look back until the corner, where he does, and lifts the menu card like a flag. Then he's gone.*

*KAMPER stands alone at the meeting point of three roads. The gold breaks over the rooftops. The first direct sunlight of the day hits the café window behind her, and the antique county map lights up in its four soft colours, red, blue, yellow, green, every county touching its neighbours, none of them the same.*

*She turns to look at it.*

**KAMPER**: *(to herself, quietly)* Charge six minus degree. Twelve in total. Somewhere it collects.

*She touches the folded napkins in her coat pocket.*

**KAMPER**: *(cont'd)* Roots three, thirteen, seventeen, twenty-one. Show me what's different about you.

> *Kamper, inside:* Forty years I've been waiting for someone to put the hard part on a napkin. And now there are three teams... no. Six people. Two halves of one table that haven't met yet. I'll be the first email. I'll make sure it's a good one. And if my rule dies by lunchtime, I'll make sure it dies *well*.

*She takes the flat road, along the tram line, towards home. The first tram of the morning comes up behind her, and she doesn't take it. She wants the walk. She wants to count the paving stones and check whether they're properly coloured.*

*Behind her, inside The Gamut, ROSA's radio plays. The ovens light. A croissant tray slides in. On the floor by the till, two red tiles still share an edge, for now.*

*The sun is fully up.*

**END OF PLAY**

### Author's note on the rumours

Every technical "rumour" the three characters pass around comes from the project's own records as of 4 October 2026. The sources are the Structural Four Colour plan, the Gate-D candidate, the Proof Navigator tree, and the earlier wrap-up plan. Proved results are described as compiled. The Kittell numbers come from the independent replay. Gate D is described as open, because it is. The three threads at the end are the characters' own research proposals, and each one comes with its own kill condition. They are offered in the spirit of the plan's rule: a proposal must name its graph class, move relation, invariant, measure, input family, coverage statement and falsifying witness.
