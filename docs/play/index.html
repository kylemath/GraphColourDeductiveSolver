Dramatis Personae

Bucky Mid-forties. Computer scientist who spent ten years on verified compilers, then moved to the formal-methods group of a large lab. Keeps a fountain pen and a mechanical keyboard in the same bag. Believes anything worth saying can be type-checked. Is privately afraid that this belief has made him a worse listener.

Kamper Early sixties. Pure mathematician in combinatorics and topology, emerita in all but title. Has read Kempe's 1879 paper in the original journal binding and Heawood's 1890 reply more times than she will admit. Distrusts the word "obviously." Distrusts the word "almost" even more.

Appken Late thirties. Former physics PhD who became a quantitative analyst. Has run derivatives desks, risk models, and one notorious backtest that turned out to be right. Thinks in payoffs, hedges, and what happens in the tail. Talks fastest when he is least sure.

Theo The evening closer at the café. Twenty-two. Studying for a statistics exam he keeps not studying for.

Rosa The morning baker. Fifty. Has a key, a radio, and no patience for anyone still sitting at a table when the dough needs the counter.

Offstage, never seen: three colleagues of our trio, each said to be working on the same old problem. Over the evening they are called "the Lean one," "the counting one," and "the replay one." By morning these names will have stopped mattering.

The Set

A café called The Gamut, inside a converted print shop. The ceiling is high and the original iron press still stands in one corner, painted black and roped off like a museum piece. A long communal table of reclaimed oak runs down the centre. Upstage left is a smaller round table under a hanging brass lamp, half-hidden behind a tall shelf of bread baskets and jars of coffee beans. This is where the play happens.

The floor is the café's pride: hexagonal and pentagonal tiles in four colours, terracotta red, slate blue, mustard yellow and bottle green. They are laid in a pattern a local artist called "a map of nowhere." Near the till, if you look closely, two red tiles share an edge.

A large antique county map hangs in the front window, its counties hand-tinted in four soft watercolours. A chalkboard menu reads: TODAY: CARDAMOM BUN. SOUP: ROASTED SQUASH. WIFI: ask Theo.

Light moves through the night as a running clock. At rise it is early-October afternoon gold, then blue dusk, then sodium-orange streetlight through the window, then darkness lit only by the brass lamp, and finally the grey-violet of the hour before sunrise.

Prologue: The Floor

4:40 p.m. Gold light. The café is busy: laptops, a toddler, two students arguing over a problem set. Theo is behind the counter, his statistics textbook face-down beside the register.

Kamper enters first, unwinding a long scarf. She does not look for a table. She looks at the floor. She stops near the till and crouches, knees protesting, staring at the two red tiles sharing an edge.

Theo

You okay, ma'am?

Kamper

(not looking up) Your floor isn't properly coloured.

Theo

It's... tile?

Kamper

Two reds touching. There. Right where everyone's feet go. (She stands.) It's fine. It's a very human mistake. People have been making it for a hundred and fifty years.

Kamper, insideEvery time. Every café, every bathroom, every cathedral floor. I can't walk on a tiling without auditing it. Forty years of this and still no cure.

She orders a black coffee and a cardamom bun and takes the round table under the brass lamp, the one behind the bread shelf. It is the table you choose when you want to be found only by the people you're expecting.

Bucky enters, already talking into an earbud.

Bucky

(into the earbud) No, the build passed. The build passed. I'm saying a passing build isn't a reason to... yes. Yes. I'll look tonight. (He taps the earbud off, sees KAMPER, and lights up.) Kamper.

Kamper

Bucky. You look like a man who's been told a build passed.

Bucky

You heard that.

Kamper

The whole room heard it. The toddler is now sceptical of continuous integration.

He orders a flat white and sits. A moment later Appken arrives, overcoat over a hoodie, phone in hand, scrolling as he walks. He nearly collides with the iron press.

Appken

Sorry. Sorry. (to the press) Sorry. (He sees the others.) Oh good, you're both here. I thought this might be the wrong Gamut.

Bucky

There's more than one?

Appken

There's a Gamut Wellness in the strip mall. I went there first. Someone tried to sell me a crystal.

Kamper

Did you buy it?

Appken

I hedged. I bought a smaller crystal.

He orders a double espresso and a soup and sits. The brass lamp makes a warm circle around the three of them. Outside the light is beginning to slant.

Scene One: Water-Cooler Fragments

5:00 p.m. The gold has deepened. The café hums.

Appken

So. Who goes first.

Bucky

Goes first with what?

Appken

Oh, come on. You both texted me "need to talk, not on Slack." Nobody says "not on Slack" unless it's either a resignation or gossip.

Kamper

Or both.

Appken

Or both. Which is it?

A pause. Each of them waits for another to lay down a card.

Bucky, insideIf I say it first and I'm wrong, I'm the guy who got excited about a rumour. If I say it first and I'm right, I'm the guy who spread it. Either way I'd rather someone else went first.
Kamper, insideThey're both waiting for me. Fine. I'm old. I'm allowed to be the fool.
Kamper

I heard something about the Four Colour problem.

Appken puts his phone face-down. Bucky, who had been stirring his flat white, stops.

Appken

(slowly) So did I.

Bucky

(a beat) So did I.

Silence. Someone at the long table laughs at a video. The espresso machine shrieks.

Kamper

Well. That's either a coincidence or a conspiracy, and I'm too tired for a conspiracy.

Appken

Who did you hear it from?

Kamper

Not from. Around. A seminar coffee break two weeks ago. A young man I'd never met was explaining something to a postdoc, and I heard the phrase "Heawood's boundary." Nobody says "Heawood's boundary" by accident. I edged over by the biscuits and listened for as long as decency allowed.

Bucky

How long does decency allow?

Kamper

Two biscuits. Three if they're small.

Appken

What did you hear in three small biscuits?

Kamper

(hesitating) I want to be careful. I heard parts. They were talking about someone else's work, a colleague, the one who does the counting arguments. I'll call her the counting one. Apparently she has proved, in a proof assistant and compiled, that every spherical map on at most eleven vertices is four-colourable.

Bucky nods quickly. Appken frowns.

Appken

Eleven vertices? That's... I'm sorry, isn't that tiny? Couldn't you just brute-force every graph up to eleven vertices on a laptop?

Kamper

(pleased he asked) That's the point. She didn't brute-force it. That's what made me eat the third biscuit. It's a counting argument, not an enumeration.

Kamper, insideCareful. You only heard the end of the sentence. Reconstruct it, don't invent it.
Kamper

(cont'd) As I understood it, she sharpened the Euler-type bound for these maps. Instead of the usual inequality she got one with a "plus two" in a useful place. Edges plus two is at most vertices plus faces. And then...

She reaches for a paper napkin, takes a pencil from her coat pocket, and writes:

Kamper

(cont'd) Suppose every vertex has degree at least five. Each edge has two ends, so five times s is at most two e. Every face has at least three sides, so three f is at most two e. Substitute, and...

She writes quickly. Bucky leans in. Appken leans in further, competitively.

Kamper

(tapping the napkin) Twelve. If every vertex has degree at least five, you need at least twelve vertices. So below twelve, there is always a vertex of degree four or less.

Appken

And degree four or less you can always... handle?

Kamper

That's the other half. That's where Kempe comes in. The correct part of Kempe.

Bucky

(quietly, almost to himself) Gate A.

Kamper and Appken both look at him.

Kamper

What did you say?

Bucky

(caught) Gate A. It's... that's what they call it. The degree-four extension. I heard it called Gate A.

Bucky, insideWell. There goes my card.
Appken

Gate A. Like there's a Gate B.

Bucky

There's a Gate B. The eleven-vertex result is Gate B. There's a C, D, E, F.

Kamper

(sitting back) Where did you hear this?

Bucky

The Lean hack evening last Thursday. The PlaneMap people. I was there to help a student with a build problem. Mathlib version mismatch, the usual misery. Someone at the next table had a laptop open, and I saw a file called FourColorExtension.lean. Compiled. Green check. I nearly fell off my stool.

Appken

Four colour. Compiled.

Bucky

That's what I thought. That's what I thought for about six hours. Then I went home and realised I'd only seen the filename.

Kamper

(gently) And the file proves?

Bucky

The theorem in that file is called... four_color_extension. Its job is to say that if you have a four-colouring of a spherical map with one vertex deleted, and that vertex has degree at most four, you can extend the colouring to the whole map.

Kamper

That's Kempe's argument. The part he got right.

Bucky

Right. But formally. Spherically. With an actual separation lemma about alternating walks, not a picture. I heard someone call it alternating_walks_intersect. You have four neighbours in rotation order. If two opposite pairs are joined by bichromatic chains, the chains have to cross. They can't, so one of them isn't connected, and you swap.

He draws on a second napkin, faster and less tidily than Kamper. It shows a square of four dots around a centre and two crossing paths, one of them struck through.

Kamper

(examining it like a grade) Acceptable.

Bucky

High praise.

Kamper

It is. Do you know what the subtle part is?

Bucky

The subtle part is that it's a spherical theorem. It doesn't hold for graphs in general. A graph where every subgraph has a vertex of degree at most four is not automatically four-colourable. K5 is a counterexample: every vertex has degree four and it needs five colours. Somebody wrote that right into the plan, in bold, so nobody would overclaim.

Kamper, insideGood. Someone on that team has been bitten before. That's the scar tissue you want.
Appken

(who has been quiet, eating soup) Okay. Okay. I have to say something and I'm worried it's going to make me sound like an idiot.

Kamper

That's the only kind of thing worth saying at this table.

Appken

My piece is different. Mine is about the stuff that didn't work.

He wipes his mouth and pulls his phone back over. He scrolls but doesn't show them the screen.

Appken

(cont'd) I was at a dinner. Finance people, a couple of ex-academics, someone's partner who does topology. And the topology partner, very drunk on an excellent Barolo, starts telling the table about a friend, the replay one, I'll call him, who does all the computation for this group. Huge amounts of it. And the thing she kept coming back to was that they kill things. They have a list of dead conjectures, and they're proud of it.

Bucky

(nodding) The navigator tree.

Appken

The what?

Bucky

They have a website, a proof navigator. It's a tree. Every node is a claim or a sub-goal, and every node has a status: proved, in progress, exploring, blocked, killed. Killed nodes stay in the tree. They don't delete them.

Appken

(delighted) Like a trade blotter where you keep the losing trades on the screen.

Kamper

Like a proper notebook.

Appken

Right. So, according to my Barolo source, there was a conjecture called "swap sufficiency." Something about only needing swaps among four of five colours...

Kamper

Using only colours one to four, never touching the fifth, when going from five colours to four.

Appken

That. Dead at nine vertices. Forty-eight counterexamples.

Kamper

(quietly) Good. Good that they found them.

Appken

And something called "surface tension rigidity," which I assumed was a cocktail, also dead. And a whole branch involving topological quantum field theory, which was dead because some symbols had the wrong signs.

Bucky

Six-j symbols.

Appken

That, yes. Then she said the thing that's been rattling round my head for a week. She said, "And the whole constructive proof is ninety-five percent done."

A beat. Kamper puts her cup down very carefully.

Kamper, insideThere it is. Ninety-five percent. That number has been said about this problem in every decade since 1879. Somewhere a ghost in a frock coat is nodding.
Bucky, insideNinety-five percent of what, measured how? Lines of proof? Lemmas? Sorries?
Appken, insideThey both flinched. Why did they both flinch? It's a good number. If a strategy were ninety-five percent built I'd fund it.
Kamper

Ninety-five percent.

Appken

That's what she said.

Kamper

When was this dinner?

Appken

Last month. But she was quoting something older. A wrap-up document, I think. Spring.

Kamper

(carefully) Then I suspect she was quoting a document that said the remaining five percent was a single lemma. And that the same document added, in the next sentence, that the last five percent might be as hard as the whole theorem.

Appken

(slowly) ...How did you know that?

Kamper

Because anyone honest would have written it.

A pause. Appken laughs, a little embarrassed.

Appken

She did say that too. I didn't put it on the blotter.

Bucky

Selective memory.

Appken

Survivorship bias. It's a professional hazard. The ninety-five survives, the caveat dies.

Kamper

The caveat always dies first. It doesn't fit in a tweet.

Theo arrives with a refill of water and glances at the napkins.

Theo

Is that... homework?

Kamper

Something like that.

Theo

Good luck. (He goes.)

Theo, insideThree grown adults drawing dots on napkins at five o'clock on a Tuesday. I'm going to be like that one day. Or I'm going to fail statistics.

Scene Two: The Pieces on the Table

5:45 p.m. Blue dusk has begun. The long table is half-empty. The toddler has gone. The window map has dimmed to grey and yellow.

Appken

Okay. I want to organise this. If this were a desk meeting I'd put up a whiteboard. Can we... can somebody list what's real?

Bucky

"Real" meaning compiled?

Kamper

"Real" meaning true. Compiled is a subset.

Bucky

Compiled is the subset I can check.

Appken

Fine. Three columns. (He takes a fresh napkin.) Compiled. Computed. Rumoured.

He draws the columns. This becomes the most important napkin of the night, though none of them knows it yet.

Bucky

Compiled. One: they rebuilt plane maps inside Lean on top of Mathlib. Retooled it. There's a structure called SphericalMap: a finite simple graph, a cyclic rotation at each vertex, and a "filling" property.

Appken

What's filling?

Bucky

(hesitating) I... think it's the condition that makes it a sphere and not some other surface. Something about even sets of edges bounding regions.

Kamper

Mod-two cycles bound. Every even edge-combination is a boundary of faces. That's what makes a sphere a sphere combinatorially: no handles for a cycle to wrap around.

Bucky

Yes. That. And the important part is that they don't assume planarity as an axiom. They construct these maps, and they prove the filling property for the constructed ones. No oracle.

Bucky, insideThat's the part that impressed me most, and I can't make either of them feel it. Not assuming the planar oracle. Ten years ago half the formalisations I reviewed hid their hardest lemma in an axiom and called it a "standard fact."
Appken

Two?

Bucky

Two: the Five Colour Theorem compiles. For spherical maps, for their generated plane maps. And I heard the axiom audit comes back with just the three standard ones: propositional extensionality, choice, quotient soundness. Nothing extra smuggled in.

Kamper

Kempe's theorem, with Heawood's correction, done right.

Bucky

Done right. Three: Gate A, degree-four extension, compiled. Four: Gate B, the eleven-vertex result, compiled. The rank bound, elimination certificates, all of it. There's even a certificate whose length is bounded by the number of edges.

Appken

(writing) Five?

Bucky

Five is a small thing, but I like it. An executable Kempe swap. Real code inside the proof assistant: find the component, swap the colours, and a proof that it equals the mathematical notion of reachability and preserves properness. With a cost accounting: n cubed adjacency checks for n rounds.

Kamper

Why do you like that?

Bucky

Because the whole point, the way I heard it, isn't just to prove colourings exist. The finish line is an algorithm. A polynomial-time four-colouring algorithm, proved correct inside Lean, that you can actually run. Then they race it against DSATUR, against SAT solvers, against genetic algorithms and random search.

Appken stops writing.

Appken

Wait. They want to benchmark it?

Bucky

That's the finish line as I heard it. Not "a proof exists." A proof that gives you the colouring, step by step, with a proof that it terminates in polynomially many steps. Then actually benchmark it.

Appken

(grinning) That's a backtest. They want a proof with a backtest.

Kamper

(dry) Appel and Haken had a backtest. It took twelve hundred hours on a mainframe.

Appken

Different backtest. They backtested the proof. This one is backtesting the colouring.

Kamper, insideHe's right, and it's a distinction I hadn't let myself make. Appel–Haken, Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas, Gonthier: all of them say "the configurations are reducible, and here is a machine that checks each one." None of them says "here is a rule you could teach a child, and the rule always finishes." That's what I miss when I teach it. You can't explain the 1976 proof to a sixteen-year-old. You can only explain what it checks.
Appken

Okay. Compiled column: five things. Computed column. That's mine, mostly. (He looks at his phone.) The replay one. The killed conjectures I've given you. Then the one that's been eating my week.

Bucky

Kittell.

Appken looks up sharply.

Appken

You heard about Kittell?

Bucky

I heard the word. I didn't know what it meant. Someone at the hack night said, "Did it survive Kittell?" the way you'd ask if a startup survived a recession.

Kamper

Kittell's graph. Twenty-three vertices. It's a famous one: a planar graph built to show that a certain naive Kempe-swap strategy can loop forever.

Appken

(fast now) Right. And the replay one did, from scratch, an independent replay: took the graph, checked planarity, and for each degree-five vertex (there are fifteen) deleted it, enumerated every proper four-colouring of what was left, up to renaming colours, and then built the whole graph of Kempe swaps between those colourings.

Bucky

Every colouring?

Appken

Six thousand three hundred and fifty root-colouring states across the fifteen roots. And the good news first, which I'm told is unusual for this group...

Kamper

(smiling despite herself) Go on.

Appken

Every root's swap graph was connected. And from every state, you can reach a colouring where the five neighbours of the deleted vertex use only three colours. That means a colour is free and you can put the vertex back. And in at most four moves. Every single one.

A pause.

Bucky

That's... that's good.

Appken

That's good. Now the bad news. Which, honestly, is the interesting part.

Appken, insideThis is the part I've been trying to explain to my wife for a week. She nodded at the right places, but I could see her pricing my sanity.
Appken

(cont'd) Suppose you want a rule. Not a search, a rule. "When you see this, do that." The natural thing is to look only at the boundary: the five neighbours, their colours, and for each pair of colours, which neighbours are connected to which by chains of that pair. Six colour pairs, so six partitions of the five neighbours. That's your signature. They called it sigma.

Kamper

The boundary information. What Kempe himself looked at.

Appken

Right. Across all fifteen roots there are forty distinct sigmas. Forty situations. And here's the first problem: two concrete colourings with the same sigma can have different sets of next sigmas. Sigma isn't a complete description. Two people can look at the same screen and be in different markets.

Bucky

The abstraction isn't a bisimulation.

Appken

I don't know what that word means, but I believe you.

Bucky

It means the coarse view doesn't predict the fine view's next step.

Appken

Then yes, exactly that. So they played a game. It's a robust game. You must choose one boundary action, a colour pair and which boundary vertices you swap through, that works for every concrete colouring hiding behind that sigma. Then they did backward induction. Start from the target signatures, the winners. Add any signature that has an action all of whose possible results are already winners. Repeat until nothing changes.

Kamper

The attractor.

Appken

The robust attractor. It captures thirty-five of the forty. Five lose. Five sigmas from which no single boundary-only rule is guaranteed to work.

He writes it in the "Computed" column:

Appken

(cont'd) So: the colourings are fine, but the rule that only looks at the boundary isn't. And I want to be careful, because she was very careful at the dinner even drunk: it doesn't say the theorem is false. It says that kind of rule can't be the whole story.

Kamper

That kind of rule is Kempe's kind of rule. That's 1879 failing again, in a new costume.

Appken

It gets better. If you don't pool the roots, if you play the game separately for each root, eleven of the fifteen roots have zero losing signatures. Perfect. Four roots still lose five each. Roots three, thirteen, seventeen, twenty-one.

Bucky

So which root you choose matters.

Appken

Which root you choose matters.

They sit with that. The light outside has gone fully blue. Theo switches on the overhead pendants. The brass lamp is now one warm circle among several.

Kamper

(after a while) And the "Rumoured" column?

Appken

(looking at it, empty) I was going to put "ninety-five percent."

Kamper

Don't.

Appken

(crossing out a pencil mark that isn't there) I won't.

Scene Three: The Dawning

6:40 p.m. Full dark outside. The long table is mostly empty now: one student asleep on her textbook, a couple sharing a slice of cake. The window map is lit only by the streetlamp, its four watercolours turned to four greys.

Bucky has his laptop out but hasn't opened it. Kamper is turning the "Compiled / Computed" napkin around with one finger.

Kamper

Let me say back what I think we have, and you tell me where I'm lying.

Appken

Deal.

Kamper

Degree four or less, compiled. Fewer than twelve vertices, compiled. Five colours, compiled, the honest way. So any counterexample to the Four Colour Theorem, any smallest one, would have to be a sphere where every vertex has degree at least five, with at least twelve vertices.

Bucky

Right.

Kamper

That's not new. Heawood knew that in 1890. That's the boundary that broke Kempe. It's where I'd expect the boundary to be.

Bucky

(quietly) "Heawood's boundary."

Kamper

Which is the phrase that made me eat three biscuits. Because the young man said it in a particular way. Not "we're stuck at Heawood's boundary," but "the verified advance reaches Heawood's boundary." As in: everything up to here is now machine-checked, with no gaps, by people who wrote down what they didn't know. And the next step is new mathematics.

Appken

So the ninety-five percent...

Kamper

Is a matter of what you're counting. If you count the infrastructure, the build, the definitions, the lemmas, maybe it's ninety-five. If you count the idea, you've got everything Kempe and Heawood had, rebuilt to modern standards, and the cliff is exactly where it was.

Appken, insideThat's a sobering re-mark. My position is the same but my valuation just dropped forty percent. No, it didn't drop. I mis-marked it in the first place.
Bucky

But it's not exactly where it was.

Kamper raises an eyebrow.

Bucky

(cont'd, opening his laptop) I mean, the cliff is the same height. But Kempe and Heawood didn't know what shape the next step had to be. Here, I think they've written down the shape. That's what Gate D is.

Appken

Okay, so what's Gate D? You keep saying gates like I'm supposed to know the floor plan.

Bucky

I only saw the table of gates on someone's screen. Let me try to recall it honestly. (He types briefly, then stops himself.) No, I'm not going to look it up. I'll say what I remember and you two correct me.

Kamper

That's the spirit of the evening.

Bucky

A is degree four. B is the twelve-vertex thing. C is "boundary semantics": defining exactly what information you're allowed to look at around one candidate configuration, and what moves are legal. D is the hard one: a uniform reduction with a progress measure. E is "general theorem, plus executable, plus polynomial." F is getting from ordinary planar graphs, the kind you'd draw, to their spherical maps.

Appken

And D is the gap.

Bucky

D is the gap. I remember the line: "this is the mathematical four-colour gap." It said that in the table.

Kamper pulls the napkin back and turns it over. On the clean side she writes, slowly:

Kamper

Is that it?

Appken

That's the game they replayed on Kittell. Yes.

Kamper

And what do they want to prove about it?

Bucky

This is the part I remember most clearly because it gave me chills. It's quantified. There are fixed constants, C and k, and two procedures. Select looks at T and picks a root. Step looks at the current state and makes a move. And for every triangulation in the class, and for every four-colouring of T minus the selected root, Step reaches a target in at most C times n to the k moves.

He writes it under Kamper's block.

A long silence. Appken reads it twice.

Appken

That's stronger than the theorem.

Kamper

(pleased) Yes.

Appken

That's a lot stronger than the theorem. The theorem just needs one good colouring. This says every colouring of the smaller graph can be walked to a good one, quickly, by a rule.

Bucky

They wrote that too. "A root-selection strengthening." They're clear that if it fails, the Four Colour Theorem doesn't fail. Only this route does. Then they'd fall back to a weaker version.

Kamper

Which is?

Bucky

Something about certificates. Instead of "every colouring works," the recursion hands you a colouring plus a certificate that it's in the winning region. The certificate has to be preserved by the recursion, and its existence has to be proved, not assumed. They had a line about circularity: if your invariant is just "this colouring is extendible," you've smuggled the theorem in as a premise.

Kamper

(nodding slowly) Yes. That's the trap. That's always the trap.

Appken

And the "progress measure" part?

Bucky

Each move has to make a number go down. Actually a pair of numbers, (p, q), compared lexicographically. P is coarse, some structural potential. Q is a finer rank to make progress on plateaus where p doesn't move. Both bounded by a polynomial. And...

Kamper

And you can't use "distance to the target" as the rank.

Bucky

(surprised) How did you know?

Kamper

Because it's circular. Distance to a target is only finite if the target is reachable, which is what you're trying to prove. And computing it means searching every colouring, which is exponential. It's the first thing a clever student would suggest, and the first thing an honest referee would reject.

Appken

(slowly, half to himself) "A rank equal to distance is circular until reachability is proved."

Bucky

Was that a quote?

Appken

I... think the Barolo woman said it? I think she was quoting. I didn't understand it at the time.

Appken, insideThat's the line I wrote on my hand in the taxi and then washed off. God, I washed it off.

Kamper sits back. She looks at the three napkins in a row: the twelve-vertex counting, Bucky's crossing chains, and now Gate D.

Kamper

I want to say something, and I want you both to stop me if it's sentimental.

Bucky

We'll stop you.

Kamper

For a hundred and fifty years the problem has been "is this true?" Since 1976 we've known it's true. Since 2005 we've known it's true to the standard of a proof assistant. What nobody has had is a why that fits in a head. What these people have done, the three of them, independently, in pieces, is reduce the problem of why to something that fits on a napkin.

She taps the Gate D napkin.

Kamper

(cont'd) That's not ninety-five percent. That's not any percent. But it's the first time in my life I've seen the hard part isolated. Clean. Quantified. With a known fixture to test against and a list of the things that don't count.

Appken

That's not sentimental.

Kamper

It's a little sentimental.

Bucky

It's a little sentimental.

They're quiet. Theo passes with a cloth, wiping the empty long table. He glances at their corner.

Theo

We close at eleven, just so you know. Kitchen closes at eight if you want food.

Appken

(not looking up) Three more soups.

Theo

Three soups. (He goes.)

Bucky

I didn't want soup.

Appken

You're going to want soup.

Bucky, insideHe's right. I'm going to want soup. I'm not going home.

Pause. Then Bucky says it.

Bucky

They're close.

Kamper

They're at the cliff.

Bucky

Well. If we're not locked in, there's no hurry.

Bucky laughs, the tired laugh of someone who's past being tired. Appken puts his head down on his arms for a moment, then lifts it again.

Appken

Okay. Then let's write down the plan.

iv. The Plan

4:30 a.m. The menu card, flipped to a fresh section, has become the master document. Bucky writes; the others dictate. The handwriting deteriorates as the list goes on.

Bucky

Rules for the plan. Theirs, basically. Each proposal must name: graph class, move relation, invariant, measure, input colouring family, coverage statement, and a falsifying witness. I'm stealing that. It's the best checklist I've heard in years.

Kamper

Steal it. Credit it.

Bucky

Credit it. Okay. Three threads.

He writes. Kamper and Appken read over his shoulders, so the stage picture is three heads bent into one circle of light.

Bucky puts down the pen. They look at it.

Appken

That's a good term sheet.

Kamper

That's a good research plan. It's honest about where it dies.

Bucky

Should we... send this to them? The three of them?

A pause. The real question of the evening.

Kamper

Not tonight. Not yet. We send them results. Even negative ones. Especially negative ones. A list of ideas from three people at a café is a list of ideas from three people at a café. They have plenty of ideas. What they don't have is three more people who have already killed some of them.

Appken

Bring them a dead conjecture, not a live hunch.

Kamper

Bring them a dead conjecture, properly labelled. That's the most useful gift in mathematics.

Bucky, insideThere it is. The thing I came here to learn without knowing it. Not how to be right. How to hand someone a well-made failure.

v. Five-Forty

5:40 a.m. Without anyone noticing, the black rectangle of the window has become very dark blue. The streetlamp outside clicks off on its timer. The window map's four watercolours are faintly, faintly visible again, as shapes if not yet colours.

Kamper is asleep, upright, scarf around her chin. Appken is drawing a small triangulated sphere on the last napkin. Bucky is watching him.

Bucky

(whispering) What's that?

Appken

(whispering) Icosahedron. I've never drawn one. I wanted to see it.

Bucky

Every vertex looks the same.

Appken

Every vertex looks the same. Nowhere for charge to hide. If our rule works here, it works because Step is good, not because we were clever about where to stand.

Bucky

That's a fair test.

Appken

It's the fairest test there is. It's the market with no information.

He writes tiny numbers on the twelve vertices, then colours a few with a pen: R, B, Y, G.

Scene Six: Rosa

6:52 a.m. The window is grey-violet and brightening. The four watercolours of the county map can now be told apart. A key turns in the BACK door. A radio, playing an old soul song, enters before anyone does. Then Rosa: flour-dusted apron, canvas bag, hair tied back. She flicks on the kitchen lights, which spill out through the swinging door, sets down her bag, and walks into the front of the café to turn on the ovens' display lights. She stops.

Three people at the round table under the brass lamp. One asleep. Two with pens. Eleven napkins, two receipts, a menu card, a stale bun.

A long beat.

Rosa

(not a scream exactly, but close) ...WHAT.

Kamper wakes with a jolt. Appken drops his pen. Bucky stands up so fast his chair tips back against the bread shelf, which shakes; one jar of coffee beans rocks and settles.

Bucky

Good morning!

Rosa

Good morning? How did you... we're closed. We've been closed since eleven. Did you break in?

Appken

We were already in.

Rosa

That's worse.

Kamper

(rising with dignity, adjusting her scarf) Madam, I apologise sincerely. The young man, Theo, very kindly told us to let ourselves out. We... failed to.

Rosa

For eight hours?

Kamper

(looking at the window, and for the first time seeing the morning) ...Apparently.

Rosa

(looking at the napkins) What is all this?

A beat. The three look at each other.

Appken

A term sheet.

Bucky

A research plan.

Kamper

A floor.

Rosa stares at them. Her radio plays on in the kitchen.

Rosa

Out. All three. I've got forty croissants to laminate and I need this table. Out.

They gather the napkins frantically. Kamper folds them into her coat pocket with the care of someone handling a manuscript. Appken rescues the menu card. Bucky shuts his laptop and tries to put the chair back down, then realises he should put it up on the table like all the others, then can't decide, and Rosa takes it out of his hands and does it herself.

They file towards the front door. Kamper stops on the floor near the till, at the two red tiles that share an edge.

Kamper

Oh. Rosa, one more thing...

Rosa

OUT.

Kamper

Those two red tiles. If you ever retile, it's a single swap. Red and blue, on the little red-and-blue patch that contains the left one. Just that piece. It doesn't reach the other red tile, and the rest of the floor won't notice. I checked last night, while Appken was talking about bonds.

Rosa

(arms folded, flour on her elbows) I'll tell the owner.

Kamper

Thank you.

Rosa, insideEvery year it's someone. Last year it was a poet who wrote on the bathroom wall. At least these ones only want to fix the floor.

The front door opens. Cold air. The first bird. The three step out onto the pavement. The door closes and locks behind them.

Epilogue: Three Roads

7:02 a.m. The street outside The Gamut. The café sits on a corner where three streets meet at uneven angles: one runs uphill towards the university, one runs downhill towards the river and the financial district, and one runs flat along the tram line towards the old residential blocks. Above the rooftops to the east, a line of bright gold is about to break. The sky is the colour that has no name, between violet and blue and grey.

The three stand in the middle of the meeting point, blinking.

Appken

(looking at the three streets) Degree three.

Kamper

Hm?

Appken

This corner. Three roads. It's a vertex of degree three. The easy case.

Kamper

(smiling, exhausted) Any colour's free.

Bucky

Tait would like it. It's cubic.

They laugh, too much, the way you laugh at seven in the morning when you haven't slept.

A pause. Nobody wants to be the first to go.

Bucky

So. Email.

Appken

Email.

Kamper

Today.

Bucky

Today. Results, not hunches. Even if it's "my thing is dead."

Appken

Especially if it's "my thing is dead."

Kamper

And properly labelled: the class, the move, the measure, the witness.

Bucky

The checklist.

Kamper

The checklist.

Bucky takes a step towards the uphill road, towards the university and its lab, then turns back.

Bucky

I'm going to refine sigma. The nesting of the chain-curves around the root, the non-crossing structure, nothing else. Then I'll run the robust game on Kittell, pooled and per-root. If those five losers turn into winners and the extra data stays bounded, I'll go straight to the icosahedron. If they don't, I'll tell you exactly which collision survived, and why.

Bucky, insideI want to be right. No. I want to know. Those aren't the same. Remember they aren't the same.
Bucky

(cont'd, aloud, to both) I'm terrified it'll work on the first try. Then I won't trust it.

Appken

That's the right thing to be terrified of.

Bucky nods, turns, and walks uphill. He walks fast, then faster, laptop bag bumping against his hip. Near the top he takes out his phone and starts typing, and stops walking to type, and starts again.

Appken turns towards the downhill road, towards the river and the glass towers, then hesitates.

Appken

I'm going to price it. The pair, p and q. Not distance, nothing circular, something I could compute from what's on the board. I'll take every Kittell state and check whether every non-target state has a swap that brings the price down. And I'm going to plot the term structure: worst-case moves against size, for every small triangulation where every vertex has degree at least five. If the curve is flat, I'll know what kind of formula to look for.

Kamper

And if it's not flat?

Appken

Then I'll know what kind of formula not to look for. That's worth just as much. Half of finance is knowing which models to never use again.

Appken, insideI'm going to call in sick. I've never called in sick. I'm going to call in sick and price a colouring.
Appken

(cont'd) I'm so nervous I feel like I'm about to go on a trading floor for the first time. That's... that's a good feeling. I'd forgotten it was a good feeling.

He walks downhill, overcoat over one arm, and doesn't look back until the corner, where he does, and lifts the menu card like a flag. Then he's gone.

Kamper stands alone at the meeting point of three roads. The gold breaks over the rooftops. The first direct sunlight of the day hits the café window behind her, and the antique county map lights up in its four soft colours, red, blue, yellow, green, every county touching its neighbours, none of them the same.

She turns to look at it.

Kamper

(to herself, quietly) Charge six minus degree. Twelve in total. Somewhere it collects.

She touches the folded napkins in her coat pocket.

Kamper

(cont'd) Roots three, thirteen, seventeen, twenty-one. Show me what's different about you.

Kamper, insideForty years I've been waiting for someone to put the hard part on a napkin. And now there are three teams... no. Six people. Two halves of one table that haven't met yet. I'll be the first email. I'll make sure it's a good one. And if my rule dies by lunchtime, I'll make sure it dies well.

She takes the flat road, along the tram line, towards home. The first tram of the morning comes up behind her, and she doesn't take it. She wants the walk. She wants to count the paving stones and check whether they're properly coloured.

Behind her, inside The Gamut, Rosa's radio plays. The ovens light. A croissant tray slides in. On the floor by the till, two red tiles still share an edge, for now.

The sun is fully up.

END OF PLAY

Author's note on the rumours

Every technical "rumour" the three characters pass around comes from the project's own records as of 4 October 2026. The sources are the Structural Four Colour plan, the Gate-D candidate, the Proof Navigator tree, and the earlier wrap-up plan. Proved results are described as compiled. The Kittell numbers come from the independent replay. Gate D is described as open, because it is. The three threads at the end are the characters' own research proposals, and each one comes with its own kill condition. They are offered in the spirit of the plan's rule: a proposal must name its graph class, move relation, invariant, measure, input family, coverage statement and falsifying witness.
