Fullerene duals · the Birkhoff diamond · one rotating cycle

Cages and Diamonds

Delete a degree-five vertex (the hole) and four-colour what is left. The hole can be filled once its five neighbours (its link) use at most three colours. A Kempe swap picks two colours and exchanges them on one whole connected two-colour component. The radius ρ of a colouring is the fewest swaps that reach a filled colouring. A colouring is doubly locked when no single swap fills it. The proof route needs only Lemma R*: in every core triangulation (4-connected, minimum degree 5) some degree-five vertex has every Kempe class of T − v containing a filled colouring [open].

Classical proofs of the Four Colour Theorem avoid a global argument like R* by listing reducible configurations. This page tells three linked stories. First, some graphs seem to have no hard holes at all: the duals of fullerene molecules. Second, they contain none of the classical configurations, and the diamond shows how a classical reduction works. Third, in a graph that does contain diamonds there is one hole where a single cycle of colourings rotates. The earlier pages are The Six-Ring Trap, The Tidy Trap and The Dead-End Region.

Story 1

Cages: fullerene duals

A fullerene dual is a triangulation with only degree-five and degree-six vertices. Euler's formula forces exactly twelve of degree five. IPR (isolated pentagon rule) means no two degree-five vertices are adjacent. These are the duals of carbon fullerenes: the C60 dual has 32 vertices and the C70 dual has 37. In an IPR cage every neighbour of a hole has degree six, so every hole is a (6,6,6,6,6) hole, the case of The Six-Ring Trap [computed here]. A cage contains no Birkhoff diamond and no RSST configuration 2.122, because both need adjacent degree-five vertices. Call such a graph configuration-free [computed here: 0 and 0].

Studio results so far [exploratory, Mac Studio; not audited]

  • IPR duals of 32 to 52 vertices (backgroundMaterial/planemap-structural/studiointel/ipr/ipr_32_52.pc, run by studiointel/ipr_run.py): 10,275 of 15,204 holes done. ρ = 2 at 38 holes, ρ = 3 at 10,214, ρ = 4 at 23, and never ρ = 5. No hole has a class that can never be filled.
  • Tabu search over configuration-free graphs with 37 to 42 vertices reached at best ρ = 4.
  • Radius 5 first appears at order 22. Every ρ = 5 hole found so far lies in a graph with a Birkhoff diamond or 2.122. One example: order 23, graph 742, hole 11, link degrees (5,6,5,7,5), a graph with 2 diamonds.
  • In the exhaustive census (backgroundMaterial/planemap-structural/longtable/studio-explore/kempe-census/README.md, Studio commit 2ea1a3f), no hole of radius 4 or more occurs in a graph with neither a diamond nor 2.122, through order 25. Such graphs are rare there: 1, 1, 4 and 2 graphs at orders 22 to 25, all with ρ ≤ 3. Configuration-free radius 4 is reported only at larger order (the C70 dual, and the tabu runs).

What this shows

Cages are easy so far

At every hole of both cages the worst colouring needs only a few swaps (see the bars). No colouring is stuck forever, and a cage is one Kempe class at each hole it was checked at [computed]. The Studio numbers above say the same for most IPR duals up to 52 vertices, and ρ = 5 never appears [exploratory]. This is finite evidence about a small family of very symmetric graphs. Story 2

The Birkhoff diamond

The Birkhoff diamond (Birkhoff, 1913) is the classic reducible configuration. Four vertices of degree five form two triangles that share an edge. Its two centres touch each other and both tips; the tips do not touch. Cut it out and what surrounds it is a ring of six vertices. A configuration is D-reducible when every proper colouring of the ring either extends directly to the four inside vertices, or Kempe swaps outside the ring turn it into one that does, whatever the outside looks like. Then a smallest counterexample cannot contain the configuration. The same holds for RSST configuration 2.122 (Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas, 1997), which is the diamond with one centre raised to degree six. Tilley (JGAA, 2017) studies the related notion of D-resolvability.

The hybrid idea · exploratory, not proved

Do the classical part the classical way

Take a smallest counterexample T to the Four Colour Theorem. Remove from the picture everything that classical reducibility already handles: separating cycles of length 3 and 4, the diamond, 2.122 and a few more. What is left has minimum degree 5 and none of these configurations. Then try the global Kempe argument (Lemma R*) at a degree-five vertex.

Math's observation [hand, unreviewed]: with no diamond, a degree-five vertex has no three consecutive degree-five neighbours, and two adjacent degree-five neighbours force a degree-six vertex beside them. So the local theorems for five-heavy holes say nothing new in this frame. All the difficulty sits in holes like (6,6,6,6,6) and (5,5,6,5,6). The excluded list must stay small and hand-provable, or nothing is left to prove.

The cages are the first weak support: configuration-free graphs where every hole fills in few swaps [exploratory]. The step from "this works in the graphs we looked at" to "it works in every graph with none of these configurations" is [open].

Story 3

The (5,5,6,5,6) hard case and its one rotating cycle

Read the five neighbours of a degree-five hole around the hole. If their degrees are 5, 5, 6, 5, 6 in cyclic order (equivalently 5, 6, 5, 6, 5), the hole is a (5,5,6,5,6) hole. It is one of the hard classes left once diamonds are excluded. In a doubly locked colouring the link repeats one colour, say α, at two positions two apart. The F move swaps the {α, c(xj+3)} chain of xj+2, the next link vertex after the repeat. The result is again a colouring of this kind, with the frame turned by three places.

Fellow F proved three facts [hand; reviewed by interns, not audited]. Lemma O: F-orbits of doubly locked colourings are finite paths, or cycles whose length is a multiple of 15. Lemma Γ: in a Kempe class with no filled colouring ("targetless") every F-cycle has length divisible by 30, and the class holds two kinds of cycle joined together, so it has at least 60 colourings. Lemma LC: a lock chain of one state is the lock chain of the next. Whether such a targetless class can exist at all (Lemma C*) is [open].

What the data show [exploratory, Studio intel]

  • The F-cycle census over 975 holes of this type (fcycle/fcycle_census_gentri16_22.json on branch origin/studio-intel) found exactly one F-cycle: order 22, hole 15, labelled length 60, 20 colourings up to renaming.
  • That class is not targetless: it contains filled colourings. The cycle's 20 states have ρ = 2 (8 states), 3 (8 states) and 4 (4 states).
  • The graph contains 5 Birkhoff diamonds, so it would be excluded by classical reduction. 8 of the 20 states have a silent first move toward a fill: a swap of a chain that touches no link vertex.

Why this is interesting

The cycle is not a trap

A targetless class would have to keep every state doubly locked forever. Here the cycle exists, but from every one of its states a short sequence of swaps reaches a filled colouring, and for 8 of the 20 the first swap does not touch the link at all. The exits sit away from the link, which is why a purely local rule at the link cannot see them. One graph with one cycle proves nothing about Lemma C*.
